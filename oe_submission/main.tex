\title{Reciprocity Constrains Angular Mixing in Passive Light-Extraction Structures: Two Spectral Metrics for OLED Outcoupling}

% ---- authors: fill in ----
\author{Author One\authormark{1}, Author Two\authormark{1} and Author Three\authormark{1,*}}
\address{\authormark{1}[Affiliation, Address]\\}
\email{\authormark{*}[corresponding@email]}


\begin{abstract*}
No common metric compares the microlens arrays, scattering layers, and textures that extract substrate modes from organic light-emitting diodes. Treating multi-pass extraction as a Markov chain on polar angle, we characterize a passive structure by two spectral quantities of its return matrix: an angular mixing number and a late-pass escape ratio. With redistribution constrained by reciprocity, the angle-resolved escape probability and the mixing number reproduce the full series within 0.30 percentage points on average over 342 cases; the memoryless formula errs by up to 60 points. Because mixing cannot open angles that a structure does not transmit, random lenses and wavy surfaces gain under 1 point, and freeform lens optimization at most 7\% in any polar band.
\end{abstract*}


\section{Introduction}

A planar OLED loses much of its generated light to waveguided modes, electrode absorption, and total internal reflection at the substrate/air interface \cite{r1,r2}. External structures extract the substrate modes without touching the electrical stack: microlens arrays (MLAs) \cite{r3,r4}, scattering layers \cite{r5,r6}, and textured films \cite{r7}. Their extraction has been mapped against lens profile, aspect ratio, and fill factor \cite{r3,r8}, and their shapes are increasingly optimized by inverse design and machine learning \cite{r9,r10}. With such structures the highest reported external quantum efficiencies of single-unit OLEDs lie near 55–62\% \cite{r5,r11,r12,r13}, a ceiling recently traced to the loss that returned light suffers on each round trip through the device \cite{r14}.

What is missing is a compact way to compare the structures themselves. Statistical ray optics assumes that light in the substrate is fully randomized on every pass \cite{r15,r16,r17}, so that $\eta = p/[p+(1-p)A']$ depends on one escape probability $p$ and one round-trip loss $A'$ \cite{r14}. Angle-resolved models propagate the power distribution from pass to pass with redistribution matrices \cite{r2,r14,r18,r19,r20,r21,r22}. They are accurate but numerical and do not separate escape from redistribution; where randomization has been quantified, it has been done empirically \cite{r23,r24,r25}. The distinction matters: a hemispherical MLA returns unextracted light largely at its original angle \cite{r14}, so later passes see mostly light at angles it transmits poorly, and the memoryless formula overestimates extraction by a structure-dependent amount.

Here we characterize a structure by the spectrum of its return matrix. The second eigenvalue gives an angular mixing number, the per-pass analogue of the transport factor $1/(1-g)$ of radiative transfer \cite{r26} and the relaxation time of a reversible chain \cite{r27}; the leading eigenvalue gives the escape probability of the quasi-stationary distribution \cite{r28}, the escape rate of open dynamical systems \cite{r29,r30,r31}. These objects, and the reciprocity that makes them real and bounded, are standard in Markov-chain, open-system, and light-transport theory \cite{r32,r33,r34}, and reciprocal coupling of a diffuse lobe to angle-dependent reflection is standard in reflectance modelling \cite{r35,r36}. What is new is their use as metrics for passive light extraction, a reciprocal reduced model of multi-pass extraction, and the resulting map of OLED outcoupling structures, which we connect to the radiance-ordered extraction bound \cite{r37,r38,r39} and test on a controlled freeform-versus-hemisphere benchmark \cite{r11,r12}.

\section{Theory}

\subsection{Multi-pass extraction as a Markov chain on angle}

The substrate angle is divided into 1\ensuremath{^\circ} polar bins $k$; $v_k$ is the power travelling towards the structure in bin $k$, integrated over azimuth, and $w_k = \sin^2\theta_{k+1}-\sin^2\theta_k$ is the bin's étendue, so uniform radiance is $v_k \propto w_k$. On each encounter a fraction $p_k$ escapes and $B_R(i\to r)$ returns from bin $i$ to bin $r$, so that

\begin{equation}
B_R = M\,\mathrm{diag}(1-\mathbf{p}),
\end{equation}

where the column-stochastic $M$ is the distribution of the returned angle conditioned on return, the transition matrix of a Markov chain on angle. The escape probability for uniform radiance is $p = \sum_k w_k p_k/\sum_k w_k$. The device reflects the returned light with its angle-resolved reflectance $R(\theta)$, and the substrate-to-air extraction efficiency is

\begin{equation}
\eta_{\mathrm{sta}} = \mathbf{p}^{\mathsf T}\left(I - \mathrm{diag}\,\mathbf{R}\; B_R\right)^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{(0)} ,
\end{equation}

with $\mathbf{v}^{(0)}$ the power launched into the substrate \cite{r14}. All losses enter through $R(\theta)$; the scalar $A' = 1-\sum_k w_k R_k/\sum_k w_k$ is used only in the memoryless comparison. The model is scalar and averaged over position and azimuth (Note S1).

\subsection{Reciprocity and the angular mixing number}

For a passive, reciprocal structure, $w_i B_R(i\to j) = w_j B_R(j\to i)$ \cite{r40}, as noted for the redistribution matrices of textured sheets \cite{r19}; it is the discrete form of the self-adjointness of reciprocal scattering operators in the étendue inner product \cite{r32}. $M$ is therefore similar to a symmetric matrix, its spectrum is real \cite{r33}, and its stationary distribution is $\propto w(1-p)$. The eigenvalue of second-largest modulus, $\lambda_2$, sets how fast the angle is forgotten, and we define

\begin{equation}
N_{\mathrm{mix}} = \frac{1}{1-|\lambda_2|},
\end{equation}

the number of passes a structure needs to forget the incident angle: $\infty$ for a planar interface, 1 for a Lambertian scatterer. For all structures here $\lambda_2$ is real and positive. Near-grazing states carry almost no étendue but can retain angle, so the spectral metrics are evaluated on $\theta < 85\ensuremath{^\circ}$ while $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ is always computed on 0–90\ensuremath{^\circ}; the cutoff changes the absolute values but not the ranking of structures (Note S1).

\subsection{Reciprocal reduced model}

The reduced model keeps $p(\theta)$ and lets a fraction $1-m$ of the returned light keep its angle while a fraction $m$ is redistributed with a fixed distribution $q$:

\begin{equation}
B_R(\theta_i\to\theta_r) = \left[1-p(\theta_i)\right]\left[(1-m)\,\delta_{ir} + m\,q(\theta_r)\right].
\end{equation}

Detailed balance holds only if $q \propto w(1-p)$: light is redistributed into each angle in proportion to how much that angle itself returns, as in reciprocal reflectance models that couple a diffuse lobe to angle-dependent specular reflection \cite{r35,r36}. To our knowledge this has not been imposed in multi-pass extraction models, which redistribute isotropically \cite{r15,r16,r17,r41}. Isotropic redistribution is reciprocal inside a separate scattering layer, but applied to the combined return of a structure whose escape depends on angle it sends returned light into an escape cone that returns almost nothing. The normalized kernel has eigenvalues 1 and $1-m$, so $m = 1-\lambda_2$.

\subsection{Late-pass escape ratio}

$\lambda_2$ tells how fast the angle is forgotten, not where the light ends up. Light that circulates many times approaches the leading eigenvector of $B_R$, the quasi-stationary distribution \cite{r28}, and escapes with probability $p_\infty = 1-\rho(B_R)$ per encounter, the escape rate of an open system \cite{r29,r30,r31}. Self-adjointness and the Rayleigh quotient with the uniform-radiance vector, together with the column sums \cite{r42}, give

\begin{equation}
\min_\theta p(\theta) \le p_\infty \le \tilde p ,
\end{equation}

where $\tilde p$ is the uniform-radiance probability of leaving the truncated chain (escape, or return beyond the cutoff; $\tilde p - p < 0.008$). We call $\xi = p_\infty/\tilde p$ the late-pass escape ratio: 1 when long-circulating light escapes as easily as Lambertian light, 0 when some angles never escape and never mix. All structures satisfy the bounds numerically (Note S1).

\subsection{Band-resolved radiance-ordered bound}

The power that a passive structure can send into an output band in one encounter is bounded by filling the band's étendue with the brightest internal radiance first \cite{r37,r38,r39}; for uniform radiance this is the étendue ratio, $1/n^2$ for the hemisphere \cite{r15}. We evaluate it per polar band with the actual first-pass radiance of the microcavity (Note S1).

\section{Methods}

\textbf{Devices.} Dipole emission and stack reflectance are computed with the Chance–Prock–Silbey model \cite{r43} extended to birefringent layers \cite{r44,r45} and transfer matrices, with measured optical constants and an incoherent substrate \cite{r46}. The Al platform of the benchmark is Al (100 nm) / B3PyMPM (61.3 nm) / TCTA:B3PyMPM:Ir(dmppy-ph)$_2$tmd (25 nm) / TCTA (10 nm) / TAPC (97.5 nm) / ITO (150 nm) / glass ($n = 1.51$), with a horizontal dipole ratio of 0.865; a second stack replaces Al by Ag, and a low-loss stack follows ref. \cite{r14}. At 550 nm, $A'$ = 0.167, 0.044, and 0.026 (Table S2, Fig. S1).

\textbf{Structures.} All structures are index-matched to the substrate. The library comprises a planar interface; six non-absorbing Henyey–Greenstein \cite{r47} scattering slabs ($g$ = 0–0.9, $\tau^*$ = 0.25–4) combined with the exit face by the adding method \cite{r48}; hemispherical and freeform hexagonal MLAs, the optima of the benchmark; 17 random MLAs on a 6 \ensuremath{\times} 6 supercell with independent lenslet profiles (spread $s$), heights, or positions and radii; and five Gaussian-correlated wavy surfaces of rms slope 0.2–1.5, which resemble the buckles used on OLEDs \cite{r49}. MLAs and textures are traced with a height-field Monte-Carlo tracer that reproduces the LightTools return matrix of the hemispherical MLA (escape probability 0.4275 against 0.4274). Ray optics requires features well above the wavelength \cite{r50}; full-wave simulations of microlenses converge to ray tracing above about 5 µm \cite{r8}. Ray noise, realization, ray count, bin width, domain size, and the residual reciprocity asymmetry change $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ by at most 0.24, $\xi$ by 0.025, and $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ by 0.34 pp (Tables S5, S6).

\textbf{Freeform benchmark.} An axisymmetric freeform MLA (13 variables: spline profile, height, ETL and HTL thicknesses) is compared with an equally optimized hemispherical MLA (3 variables) on the Al platform with LightTools ray tracing validated against fabricated devices \cite{r11,r12}; the hemisphere is a point of the freeform design space (Table S10). Total EQE and the EQE in four polar bands (0–20\ensuremath{^\circ}, 20–40\ensuremath{^\circ}, 40–60\ensuremath{^\circ}, 60–80\ensuremath{^\circ}) are optimized separately by surrogate and pattern search (MATLAB surrogateopt, patternsearch). Each optimum is re-evaluated three times at full fidelity, and a restart control repeats every search from the hemispherical optimum; gains are tested with a pooled $t$ statistic and Holm correction (Note S6).

\section{Results}

\subsection{Two parameters predict multi-pass extraction}

Fig.~\ref{fig:2} compares four models with the full series for 342 cases: three devices, three wavelengths (500, 550, 600 nm), and 38 structures. The memoryless formula overestimates the planar interface by up to 60 pp, because a flat face never lets returned light into the escape cone, and the hemispherical MLA by 2–3 pp (Fig.~\ref{fig:2}a). Assuming that every ray keeps its angle fails in the opposite direction for scattering layers (Fig.~\ref{fig:2}b). With $m = 1-\lambda_2$ and isotropic redistribution the reduced model fits the lens arrays but overestimates shallow wavy surfaces by up to 10.5 pp (Fig.~\ref{fig:2}c): their $\lambda_2$ is low, yet trapped light is returned mostly to other trapped angles (from 70–80\ensuremath{^\circ}, only 3–8\% reaches angles with $p > 0.3$, against 50\% for isotropic redistribution). The reciprocal redistribution builds this in and reduces the error to 0.30 pp on average and 3.0 pp at most, without an additional parameter (Fig.~\ref{fig:2}d); the error is not concentrated in any device or wavelength (Fig.~\ref{fig:2}e, Table S4). A structure is thus described by $p(\theta)$ and one number for how fast it forgets the angle, provided the mixing respects reciprocity.

\subsection{The structure map}

On the $(p, N_{\mathrm{mix}})$ plane the classes separate (Fig.~\ref{fig:3}a). The planar interface has $p$ = 0.40 and $N_{\mathrm{mix}} = \infty$ ($\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ = 0.38 on Al). Scattering layers mix within 1.1–2.3 passes but escape less, $p$ = 0.19–0.39 ($\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ = 0.58–0.75). Hemispherical MLAs have the highest escape probability, $p$ = 0.42, but $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ = 6–8 ($\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ = 0.78–0.79); freeform MLAs share $p$ = 0.42–0.43 with $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ = 3–7.5.

Randomness lowers $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ without lowering $p$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:3}b, Table S3). Independent lenslet profiles reduce it from 7.0 to 1.9 \ensuremath{\pm} 0.1, position jitter of 30\% of the pitch to 3.5 \ensuremath{\pm} 0.1, and height spread of \ensuremath{\pm}60\% only to 4.1 \ensuremath{\pm} 0.2. Wavy surfaces of rms slope 0.4–1.5 reach $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ = 1.1–1.2, as low as a scattering layer, with $p$ rising from 0.41 to 0.43; at slope 1.5 they give the highest $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ of all structures, 0.801 against 0.792 for the hemispherical MLA.

The late-pass escape ratio adds what $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ cannot see (Fig.~\ref{fig:3}c). For lens arrays the two are tied: $\xi$ falls from 0.80 to 0.39 as $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ rises from 2 to 8, because light that keeps its angle ends up where the lenses transmit worst; the hemispherical MLA has $\xi$ = 0.47. Random lenses reach 0.7–0.8, wavy surfaces of slope \ensuremath{\geq} 0.7 0.81–0.88, and thick scattering layers 0.93–0.99. The shallowest wavy surface ($\xi$ = 0.35) and a thin isotropic scatterer (0.54) fall below the lens trend at $N_{\mathrm{mix}} \approx 2$: they mix quickly but locally, recycling trapped light among trapped angles, and they carry the largest residual errors in Fig.~\ref{fig:2}d. $\xi$ thus flags where a single mixing rate is least reliable.

The contours show what this is worth: they run almost vertically, so $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ is set mainly by $p$. For lens, scattering-layer, and wavy templates of $p(\theta)$ alike, the whole range of $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ is worth a change of $p$ by only 0.02–0.05 (Table S7). Randomizing the lenslet profiles moves $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ from 7.0 to 1.9 but changes $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ by less than 1 pp, because the faster mixing (+0.9 pp on Al) is offset by the reshaped $p(\theta)$ (-2.2 pp; Table S8). The planar interface makes the reciprocity constraint explicit: even complete mixing at its own $p(\theta)$ raises $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ only from 0.38 to 0.49, because a face that returns almost nothing from the escape cone must, by reciprocity, send almost no mixed light back into it. Mixing cannot open angles at which a structure does not transmit; only a change of $p(\theta)$ can.

\subsection{Radiance bound and output composition}

On the first pass the microcavity launches a strongly anisotropic radiance (Fig. S1). For the Al platform at 550 nm, the radiance-ordered bound allows 0.085, 0.205, 0.230, and 0.156 of the substrate power into the 0–20\ensuremath{^\circ}, 20–40\ensuremath{^\circ}, 40–60\ensuremath{^\circ}, and 60–80\ensuremath{^\circ} bands; these are separate maxima that draw on the same bright directions, and the bound for the whole hemisphere is 0.540, against $1/n^2$ = 0.439 for uniform radiance (Fig.~\ref{fig:4}a). Every passive structure stays well below it: MLAs reach 76–80\% in total and 55–70\% per band, the wavy surface of slope 1.0 77\%, the planar interface 65\%, and scattering layers 35–68\%. The lenses transmit almost exactly what they would for uniform radiance (0.43 against 0.44), so the cavity's anisotropy is not used.

The output composition is nearly independent of the structure in lossy devices (Fig.~\ref{fig:4}b). The selectivity $S_{40\text{–}60} = P_{\mathrm{air}}(40\text{–}60\ensuremath{^\circ})/P_{\mathrm{air}}(0\text{–}90\ensuremath{^\circ})$ of the multi-pass output stays within 0.33–0.36 on Al at 550 nm, close to the Lambertian partition of 0.337, and within 0.32–0.36 at 500 and 600 nm and on Ag (Table S9). In the low-loss device it widens to 0.27–0.37, because the long angle retention of hemispherical MLAs lets late passes, shaped by the structure, dominate when little is lost per round trip.

\subsection{Case study: freeform versus an equally optimized hemisphere}

The map explains a controlled shape-optimization benchmark. Band-optimized freeform profiles differ visibly from the hemisphere (Fig.~\ref{fig:5}a), but the gains are small: $G_j$ = 1.003, 1.002, 1.070, and 1.036 for the four bands and 1.013 for total EQE (Fig.~\ref{fig:5}b, Table~\ref{tab:2}). The restart control shows that these margins reflect the design space rather than a weak search: gains at 40–60\ensuremath{^\circ}, 60–80\ensuremath{^\circ}, and total EQE are significant ($t$ = 115, 30, 69) and survive Holm correction, while the 0–40\ensuremath{^\circ} residues are +0.3\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:5}c).

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Why lens shape saturates.} A hemispherical array already escapes close to the étendue bound $1/n^2$ for uniform radiance, so profile changes cannot raise $p$ appreciably; they change $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ and the shape of $p(\theta)$, along which the efficiency contours are flat. The cavity's anisotropy would in principle allow more: a coextensive surface cannot enlarge a band's étendue, but one with binary angular transmission can reach the radiance-ordered bound \cite{r39}, and skewness conservation does not forbid this \cite{r51}. The rotationally symmetric lenses and textures studied here, however, fill each band from a broad range of internal angles and extract close to what uniform radiance would give. Approaching the band-resolved bound thus appears to call for angle-selective transmission rather than further freedom in the lens profile.

\textbf{Why mixing matters little.} Reciprocity ties mixing to escape: a structure that sends trapped light into the escape cone must return light from the cone to trapped angles in the same étendue-weighted proportion, lowering its escape for fresh light. Scattering layers show this trade-off, reaching $\xi \approx 1$ but losing $p$ with thickness, so that extraction peaks at intermediate scatterance \cite{r18,r21}. At fixed $p(\theta)$ the span between full angle retention and complete reciprocal mixing is 1.8–4.6 pp (Al), 1.7–7.0 pp (Ag), and 0.1–1.7 pp (low loss) for the lens arrays studied, and real structures already capture part of it.

\textbf{Design implications.} Three numbers are worth reporting for a new outcoupling structure: $p$, which sets the efficiency; $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$, which sets the accuracy of any memoryless description; and $\xi$, which tells whether long-circulating light is still extractable. Random MLAs and wavy surfaces of rms slope one or more combine a high $p$ with low $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ and high $\xi$, but their efficiency advantage over periodic MLAs is \ensuremath{\leq} 1 pp; irregular microlenses have been used on OLEDs mainly to avoid diffraction and colour shift \cite{r52}. Optimizers, including machine-learning ones \cite{r9,r10}, can be benchmarked against the $(p, N_{\mathrm{mix}})$ map and the band-resolved bound.

\textbf{Scope.} The analysis covers passive, angle-insensitive external structures index-matched to the substrate, in the ray-optical regime; angle-selective filters, internal structures, absorbing scatterers, and wavelength converters are outside it. As in earlier substrate models \cite{r2,r18}, the device is an angle-preserving reflector; diffuse reflection would add a non-diagonal factor whose product with $B_R$ need not keep a real spectrum \cite{r33}.

\section{Conclusion}

We characterized passive light-extraction structures by two quantities from the spectrum of their return matrix, an angular mixing number and a late-pass escape ratio. Reciprocity, a known property of passive scattering, makes the spectrum real, bounds the late-pass escape probability, and fixes how a reduced model must redistribute returned light. With this reciprocal redistribution, $p(\theta)$ and $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ predict multi-pass extraction within 0.30 pp on average across 38 planar, scattering, microlens, random-lens, and wavy structures, where the memoryless formula deviates by up to 60 pp. Because mixing cannot open angles that a structure does not transmit, the efficiency is set mainly by the escape probability: random lenses and wavy surfaces gain less than 1 pp, and freeform optimization of microlens shape mainly changes $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$, consistent with its gain of at most 7\% in any band. Going further likely calls for angle-selective rather than shape-optimized structures.
