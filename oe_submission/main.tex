\begin{abstract*}
Microlens arrays, scattering layers, and random textures are widely used to extract substrate modes from organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs), and machine-learning optimization of their shapes is increasingly common. There is, however, no common metric for comparing such structures. Closed-form models assume that the angle of the returned light is fully randomized on every pass, and angle-resolved matrix models are evaluated only numerically. Here we treat multi-pass extraction as a Markov chain on the polar angle in the substrate. The return matrix of a passive, reciprocal structure obeys detailed balance with respect to the étendue weight, so its spectrum is real. Its second eigenvalue $\lambda_2$ defines an angular mixing number $N_{\mathrm{mix}} = 1/(1-\lambda_2)$, the number of passes a structure needs to lose the angle of the incident light. A reduced model that describes each structure only by its angle-resolved escape probability $p(\theta)$ and $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ reproduces the full angle-resolved series to within 0.6 percentage points (pp) on average and 4.7 pp at most over 324 combinations of three devices, three wavelengths, and 36 planar, scattering, microlens, random-lens, and wavy structures. The memoryless closed form deviates by up to 60 pp. The reduced model fails only for shallow surfaces that combine dead angles with local, small-step mixing. On the $(p, N_{\mathrm{mix}})$ map the structure classes separate. Hemispherical microlens arrays have the highest escape probability but return light at its original angle for six to eight passes. Scattering layers forget the angle within one or two passes but escape less. Randomizing the lenslet shapes or positions, or replacing the lenses by a wavy surface of rms slope one or more, keeps the microlens escape probability while lowering $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ to 1.1–3.5. This changes the extraction efficiency by less than 1 pp: once the escape probability is high and has no dead angles, it alone sets the efficiency. Resolving the radiance-ordered extraction bound into polar bands for the anisotropic radiance of a microcavity OLED shows that every passive structure reaches only 35–80\% of it on the first pass, and that angle-insensitive structures keep the 40–60\ensuremath{^\circ} selectivity within 0.33–0.36 regardless of their type. Freeform optimization of the lens profile therefore changes $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ but hardly $p$, which explains why it gains at most 7\% in any polar band over an equally optimized hemisphere.
\end{abstract*}


\section{Introduction}

A planar OLED loses much of its generated light. Waveguided modes, losses at the metal electrode, and total internal reflection (TIR) at the substrate/air interface all trap photons \cite{r1,r2}. External structures on the substrate extract the substrate modes without touching the electrical stack: microlens arrays (MLAs) \cite{r3,r4,r5}, scattering layers \cite{r6,r7}, and textured films \cite{r8}. Their shapes are increasingly optimized by inverse design and machine learning \cite{r9,r10}. With such structures, OLED external quantum efficiencies (EQEs) have generally plateaued near 55–60\%, a ceiling recently traced to the loss that light returned by the structure suffers on each round trip through the device \cite{r11}.

What is missing is a common way to compare the structures themselves. Two descriptions are in use. The first is the statistical ray-optics picture, in which the light in the substrate is assumed to be fully randomized in angle on every pass \cite{r12,r13}. The extraction efficiency then depends on one escape probability $p$ and one round-trip loss $A'$, $\eta = p/[p+(1-p)A']$ \cite{r11}. The second propagates the angle-resolved power distribution from pass to pass with redistribution matrices, as in the OPTOS formalism for solar cells \cite{r14}, in models of textured LEDs \cite{r15}, and in the trans-scale model of ref. \cite{r11}. The matrix models are accurate but numerical, and the degree of randomization they contain is not extracted. Where randomization has been quantified, it has been done empirically: by a randomization fraction \cite{r16}, by the number of passes after which the internal distribution looks Lambertian \cite{r17}, or not at all, as noted for partial randomization in thin-film solar cells \cite{r18}.

The distinction matters for OLEDs. A hemispherical MLA returns unextracted light largely at its original angle \cite{r11}. Light at angles it transmits well leaves in the first passes, and later passes see mostly light at angles it transmits poorly. The memoryless formula then overestimates extraction, and the error depends on the structure. It also matters for the expected value of shape optimization. If a structure acts mainly through how it redistributes returned light, a freeform profile may change the redistribution without changing how much light escapes.

Here we introduce an angular mixing number, $N_{\mathrm{mix}} = 1/(1-\lambda_2)$, defined by the second eigenvalue of a structure's return matrix. It is the per-pass counterpart of the transport factor $1/(1-g)$ of radiative transfer \cite{r19} and of the relaxation time of a reversible Markov chain \cite{r20}. We show that $p(\theta)$ and $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ suffice to predict multi-pass extraction for planar, scattering, and microlens structures, place the structure classes on a common map, and connect the map to the radiance-ordered extraction bound \cite{r21,r22,r23}. As a case study, we use a controlled freeform-versus-hemisphere benchmark on an experimentally validated OLED platform \cite{r24,r25} to show why lens-shape freedom saturates.

\section{Theory}

\subsection{Multi-pass extraction as a Markov chain on angle}

Light delivered to the substrate is described by a vector $\mathbf{v}$ of power in polar-angle bins $\theta_k$. On each encounter with the structure a fraction $p(\theta_k)$ escapes into air, and the remainder returns into the substrate with the redistribution matrix $B_R(\theta_i \to \theta_r)$. The device reflects the returned light with its angle-resolved reflectance $R(\theta)$, without changing its angle. The power reaching the structure after $m$ round trips is $\mathbf{v}^{(m)} = (\mathrm{diag}\,R\; B_R)^m\,\mathbf{v}^{(0)}$, and the substrate-to-air extraction efficiency is

\begin{equation}
\eta_{\mathrm{sta}} = \mathbf{p}^{\mathsf T}\left(I - \mathrm{diag}\,R\; B_R\right)^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{(0)} ,
\end{equation}

the matrix form of the trans-scale model \cite{r11}. Normalizing each column of $B_R$ gives a column-stochastic matrix $M$, the transition matrix of a Markov chain on the polar angle of the returned light.

\subsection{Reciprocity, detailed balance, and the angular mixing number}

For a passive, reciprocal structure, the power scattered from bin $i$ into bin $j$ and from $j$ into $i$ obey $w_i\,B_R(i\to j) = w_j\,B_R(j\to i)$, where $w_k = \sin^2\theta_{k+1}-\sin^2\theta_k$ is the étendue of bin $k$. This is detailed balance with respect to the étendue weight. $M$ is therefore similar to a symmetric matrix, its eigenvalues are real, and for a lossless structure the uniform-radiance (Lambertian) distribution is stationary. The largest eigenvalue is one. The second, $\lambda_2$, sets how fast any other initial distribution relaxes towards the stationary one: the deviation shrinks by $\lambda_2$ per pass. We define the angular mixing number

\begin{equation}
N_{\mathrm{mix}} = \frac{1}{1-|\lambda_2|},
\end{equation}

the number of passes the structure needs to forget the angle of the incident light. A planar interface has $\lambda_2 = 1$ and $N_{\mathrm{mix}} = \infty$; a Lambertian scatterer has $\lambda_2 = 0$ and $N_{\mathrm{mix}} = 1$. $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ is the relaxation time of the chain \cite{r20} and the per-pass analogue of the $1/(1-g)$ scattering events that a photon needs to lose its direction in radiative transfer \cite{r19}. Because states at near-grazing angles carry almost no étendue but can return light at the same angle, we evaluate $\lambda_2$ on $\theta < 85\ensuremath{^\circ}$, which excludes 0.76\% of the étendue (Note S10).

\subsection{Reduced model}

The reduced model keeps the angle-resolved escape probability $p(\theta)$ and replaces $B_R$ by a single mixing parameter $m$: the returned light is $(1-p(\theta))[(1-m)\,\delta(\theta_r-\theta_i) + m\,w(\theta_r)/\sum w]$. With $m = 1$ and a scalar $p$, this is the memoryless formula of ref. \cite{r11}; with $m = 0$ the structure returns every ray at its own angle. We set $m = 1-\lambda_2$, so that the reduced model relaxes at the same rate as the full matrix.

\subsection{Band-resolved radiance-ordered bound}

The power that a passive structure can send into a set of output directions in one encounter is bounded by filling the corresponding output étendue with the brightest internal radiance first \cite{r21,r22,r23}. For a uniform internal radiance the bound reduces to the étendue ratio, $1/n^2$ for the full hemisphere \cite{r12} and $(\sin^2\theta_{\mathrm{hi}}-\sin^2\theta_{\mathrm{lo}})/n^2$ for a polar band in air. An OLED microcavity, however, launches an anisotropic radiance into the substrate. We resolve the bound into the four polar bands used below, with the actual first-pass radiance of the device, and use it to rate how much of the available phase space each structure uses.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Devices}

Dipole emission and the device reflectance are computed with the Chance–Prock–Silbey model extended to birefringent layers and with transfer matrices, using measured optical constants \cite{r11,r24}. Three bottom-emitting stacks are used. The first is the platform of the freeform benchmark: Al (100 nm) / B3PyMPM / TCTA:B3PyMPM:Ir(dmppy-ph)$_2$tmd (25 nm) / TCTA (10 nm) / TAPC / ITO (150 nm) / glass ($n = 1.51$), with a horizontal dipole ratio of 0.865. Its round-trip loss for Lambertian light is $A' = 0.17$ at 530 nm. The second replaces Al by Ag ($A' = 0.049$). The third is a low-loss stack, Ag / B3PyMPM (200 nm) / EML / TAPC (180 nm) / ITO (50 nm), on glass ($A' = 0.027$) \cite{r11}. The angular distribution of the light delivered to the substrate and the device reflectance are computed per wavelength.

\subsection{Structures and their return matrices}

All structures are index-matched to the substrate, so the structure/air boundary sets the escape condition. Their transmission and return matrices are computed on 1\ensuremath{^\circ} (microlenses) or 0.5\ensuremath{^\circ} (others) polar bins.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Planar interface.} Fresnel reflection, returned at the incident angle.
\item \textbf{Scattering layers.} Index-matched, non-absorbing slabs with a Henyey–Greenstein phase function (asymmetry $g$ = 0–0.9) and transport optical thickness $\tau^* = S(1-g)$ = 0.25–4, combined with the flat exit face by the adding method; Monte Carlo with 20,000 rays per incidence bin.
\item \textbf{Hexagonal MLAs.} Close-packed axisymmetric lenslets of the spline family of the benchmark (Table~\ref{tab:1}), traced with a height-field Monte-Carlo tracer that reproduces the LightTools return matrix of the hemispherical MLA (escape probability 0.4275 against 0.4274; Note S11). The set includes the hemispherical and freeform optima of the benchmark.
\item \textbf{Random MLAs.} A 6 \ensuremath{\times} 6 supercell in which every lenslet carries its own profile (shape spread $s$ about the quarter circle), its own height (aspect spread), or a jittered position and radius; overlapping lenslets take the higher surface.
\item \textbf{Wavy surfaces.} Periodic Gaussian-correlated random height fields with rms slope 0.2–1.5. Ray optics is scale-free, so only the slope distribution matters.
\end{itemize}

The random structures (22 in total) are listed with their parameters and results in Table S12. All return matrices are traced with 20,000 rays per incidence bin (10,000 for wavy surfaces); the fraction of rays lost to the iteration limit is below $6\times10^{-4}$ for every structure.

\subsection{Freeform benchmark}

The case study of Section 4.4 compares an axisymmetric freeform MLA with an equally optimized hemispherical MLA on the Al platform. LightTools ray tracing is driven from MATLAB, with the CPS source on a 1 mm-radius disc and a 1.295 mm glass substrate. The pipeline has been validated against fabricated devices with nearly the same geometry, including attached and inverted MLAs \cite{r24,r25}. Both lens classes share the material, pitch, height range, and cavity bounds (Table~\ref{tab:1}); the hemisphere is a point of the freeform design space. Total EQE and the EQE in four polar bands (0–20\ensuremath{^\circ}, 20–40\ensuremath{^\circ}, 40–60\ensuremath{^\circ}, 60–80\ensuremath{^\circ}) are optimized as separate objectives by surrogate-based global search followed by pattern search. Every optimum is re-evaluated three times with 50,000 rays and 151 wavelengths. A restart control repeats every search from the hemispherical optimum and tests the gain with a pooled $t$ statistic, with Holm correction for the five objectives (Note S2).

\section{Results}

\subsection{Two parameters predict multi-pass extraction}

Fig.~\ref{fig:2} compares three models with the full angle-resolved series for 342 combinations: three devices, three wavelengths (500, 550, and 600 nm), and 38 structures (planar, six scattering layers, nine hemispherical and freeform MLAs, 17 random MLAs, and five wavy surfaces). The memoryless formula fails for structures that retain the angle (Fig.~\ref{fig:2}a). It overestimates the planar interface by up to 60 pp, because it lets returned light reach the escape cone that a flat face never lets it reach, and it overestimates the hemispherical MLA by 2–3 pp. Over all cases its mean error is 3.9 pp. Assuming that every ray returns at its own angle fails in the opposite direction for scattering layers (Fig.~\ref{fig:2}b). With $m = 1-\lambda_2$, the reduced model reproduces the series to within 0.56 pp on average and 4.7 pp at most for 324 of the 342 cases (Fig.~\ref{fig:2}c, Table S11). The mean error is 0.38 pp for the hemispherical and freeform MLAs, 0.44 pp for the random MLAs, and 0.96 pp for the scattering layers, whose thin isotropic members mix non-uniformly across angles.

The exceptions are the two shallowest wavy surfaces (rms slope 0.2 and 0.4), where the reduced model overestimates $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ by 5.4 pp on average and by up to 10.5 pp. Their $\lambda_2$ is low, yet light trapped beyond the critical angle is returned mostly to other trapped angles: from 70–80\ensuremath{^\circ}, only 3–8\% of the returned light lands in angles with $p(\theta) > 0.3$ after one pass on the slope-0.2 surface, against 50\% for a uniform redistribution. $\lambda_2$ measures how fast the angle is forgotten, not whether returned light can reach the escape cone. A single mixing number suffices when $p(\theta)$ has no dead angles or when mixing is non-local, which holds for every lens array and scattering layer we examined and for wavy surfaces of rms slope \ensuremath{\geq} 0.7 (mean error 1.1 pp).

A structure is therefore described, for the purpose of multi-pass extraction, by its angle-resolved escape probability and one number for how fast it forgets the angle.

\subsection{The structure map}

Fig.~\ref{fig:3}a places all structures on the $(p, N_{\mathrm{mix}})$ plane, with contours of $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ for the Al device. The classes separate:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Planar interface:} $p$ = 0.40 and $N_{\mathrm{mix}} = \infty$. Light outside the escape cone never reaches it, and $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ = 0.38.
\item \textbf{Scattering layers:} $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ = 1.1–2.3, but $p$ = 0.19–0.39, falling with thickness because more light is back-scattered before it reaches the exit face. $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ = 0.58–0.75.
\item \textbf{Hemispherical MLAs:} the highest escape probability, $p$ = 0.42, but $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ = 6–8. $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ = 0.78–0.79.
\item \textbf{Freeform MLAs:} $p$ = 0.42–0.43, like the hemisphere, with $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ = 3–7.5.
\end{itemize}

Randomness lowers $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ without lowering $p$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:3}b, Table S12). Drawing every lenslet profile independently reduces $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ from 7.0 to 1.9 while $p$ stays at 0.42–0.43. Jittering the lenslet positions by up to 30\% of the pitch reduces it to 3.5, and adding a 20\% radius spread to 3.2. Varying only the lenslet heights is the least effective (7.1 \ensuremath{\rightarrow} 4.1 at \ensuremath{\pm}60\%). A wavy surface of rms slope 0.4–1.5 has $\lambda_2$ = 0.12–0.18 ($N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ = 1.1–1.2), as low as a scattering layer, and its escape probability rises with slope from 0.41 to 0.43, reaching that of the hemispherical MLA at slope 1.0. At slope 1.5 the wavy surface gives the highest $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ of all structures on the Al device, 0.801, against 0.792 for the hemispherical MLA.

The contours show what this is worth, and the answer is: little, once $p$ is high. They run almost vertically, so $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ is set mainly by $p$. The span available to mixing is bracketed by two limits of the reduced model at fixed $p(\theta)$, full angle retention ($m = 0$) and complete mixing ($m = 1$, Sec. 2.3). For the hemispherical MLA on the Al device this span is 0.780–0.817, and the real structure sits at 0.792. Randomizing the lenslet profiles moves $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ from 7.0 to 1.9 but changes $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ by less than 1 pp (0.794 \ensuremath{\rightarrow} 0.785), because the same randomness also reshapes $p(\theta)$ and sends part of the returned light to angles where escape is less likely. The planar interface is the opposite case. Its $p(\theta)$ is zero beyond the critical angle, light there is never mixed back, and the span is 0.38–0.79. Mixing pays where $p(\theta)$ has dead angles; it pays little where $p(\theta)$ is already broad (Section 5).

\subsection{How much of the radiance bound passive structures use}

On the first pass, the microcavity delivers light to the substrate with a radiance that is far from uniform. For the Al platform at 550 nm, filling each air band with the brightest internal radiance allows 0.085, 0.205, 0.230, and 0.156 of the substrate power into the 0–20\ensuremath{^\circ}, 20–40\ensuremath{^\circ}, 40–60\ensuremath{^\circ}, and 60–80\ensuremath{^\circ} bands, and 0.540 in total (Fig.~\ref{fig:4}a). The uniform-radiance bound gives 0.051, 0.130, 0.148, and 0.096, and 0.439 in total. Every passive structure we examined stays well below the band-resolved bound. The MLAs reach 76–80\% of it in total and 55–70\% per band, a wavy surface of rms slope 1.0 77\%, the planar interface 65\%, and the scattering layers 35–68\%. The lenses transmit almost exactly as much as a structure facing uniform radiance would (0.43 against 0.44), so the anisotropy that the cavity provides is not used.

The output composition, finally, is nearly independent of the structure type (Fig.~\ref{fig:4}b). Over planar, scattering, hemispherical, freeform, random, and wavy structures, the share of the output in the 40–60\ensuremath{^\circ} band stays between 0.33 and 0.36, close to the Lambertian partition of 0.337. Within the class of angle-insensitive passive structures, the angular composition of multi-pass output is therefore set by the platform rather than by the structure.

\subsection{Case study: freeform versus an equally optimized hemisphere}

The map explains the result of a controlled shape-optimization benchmark. On the Al platform, band-optimized freeform profiles differ visibly from the hemisphere and from one another (Fig.~\ref{fig:5}a), but the gains are small: $G_j$ = 1.003, 1.002, 1.070, and 1.036 for the four bands, and $G$ = 1.013 for total EQE (Fig.~\ref{fig:5}b, Table~\ref{tab:2}). The restart control shows that these margins come from the design space and not from a weak search (Fig.~\ref{fig:5}c). Gains are significant at 40–60\ensuremath{^\circ} (+4.70\%, $t$ = 115), 60–80\ensuremath{^\circ} (+3.64\%, $t$ = 30), and for total EQE (+1.01\%, $t$ = 69), and remain significant under Holm correction. At 0–40\ensuremath{^\circ} the residues are +0.32\% and +0.20\%; a five-repeat re-measurement resolves the 20–40\ensuremath{^\circ} residue as +0.29\% ($t$ = 4.1). The same procedure that resolves a +0.29\% residue does not miss gains ten times larger.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Why lens shape saturates.} The escape probability of a lens array for uniform radiance is close to the étendue bound $1/n^2$ already for a hemisphere, so profile changes cannot raise $p$ appreciably. What they can change is how the returned light is redistributed, that is, $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$, and the shape of $p(\theta)$. Along that direction the efficiency contours are flat. The anisotropy of the cavity emission would allow more, but a refractive surface that is coextensive with an extended source cannot map the bright part of the internal radiance into a chosen band; it behaves as if the radiance were uniform.

\textbf{When mixing matters.} Mixing is worth most when two conditions meet: $p(\theta)$ has dead angles, and the round trip is lossy. For a planar interface the span between full retention and complete mixing is 40 pp on the Al device and 57 pp on the Ag device, which is why scattering layers and lens arrays help planar OLEDs at all. Once a structure has removed the dead angles, the span shrinks to 3–7 pp (Al), 2–8 pp (Ag), and 0.3–2.4 pp in the low-loss device for the lens arrays we examined, and real structures already capture part of it. The random lens arrays show that the remainder is hard to collect: the randomness that lowers $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ also flattens $p(\theta)$, and the two effects nearly cancel. Lowering $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ further therefore buys at most a few percentage points, and only in lossy devices. Dead angles also limit the reduced model itself: on shallow surfaces, where trapped light is returned to other trapped angles, a second quantity, the per-pass transfer from dead to live angles, would be needed (Section 4.1). In low-loss devices the choice of outcoupling structure, and its shape in particular, matters even less, in line with ref. \cite{r11}.

\textbf{Design implications.} Two parameters are worth reporting for any new outcoupling structure: its escape probability for uniform radiance and its angular mixing number. A structure that combines a high $p$ with a low $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$, such as a random MLA or a wavy surface of rms slope one or more, avoids both the low escape probability of thick scattering layers and the angle retention of periodic lenses, without introducing absorption. Its advantage in efficiency over a periodic MLA is small (\ensuremath{\leq} 1 pp), but it is obtained with a structure that is easier to fabricate than a precisely shaped lens array, and its output is free of the diffraction and moiré that periodic arrays can produce. Optimizers, including machine-learning ones \cite{r9,r10}, can then be benchmarked against the $(p, N_{\mathrm{mix}})$ map and the band-resolved bound rather than against each other.

\textbf{Scope.} The analysis covers passive, angle-insensitive external structures index-matched to the substrate, in the ray-optical regime. Elements that select the angle of transmission, such as angular filters, are also passive and also obey the radiance bound per encounter, but in combination with recycling they can change the output composition; they are outside the present scope. Internal structures that act on waveguided modes, absorbing scatterers, and wavelength-converting layers are not covered either. The reduced model assumes that the device reflectance preserves the angle and that $p(\theta)$ is azimuthally symmetric. The three wavelengths (500, 550, and 600 nm) bracket the green emission band; the conclusions hold at each of them (Table S11).

\section{Conclusion}

We introduced an angular mixing number, $N_{\mathrm{mix}} = 1/(1-\lambda_2)$, from the spectrum of the return matrix of a passive light-extraction structure. Together with the angle-resolved escape probability it predicts multi-pass extraction to within 0.6 pp on average across 36 planar, scattering, microlens, random-lens, and wavy structures, where the memoryless formula deviates by up to 60 pp; it fails only where dead angles meet local mixing. On the resulting map, periodic microlenses combine high escape probability with long angle retention, scattering layers the opposite, and random lens arrays and wavy surfaces combine the escape probability of the former with the mixing of the latter. Because the efficiency is set mainly by the escape probability once dead angles are removed, this combination is worth less than 1 pp. Every passive structure uses only part of the band-resolved radiance bound, and angle-insensitive structures share one output composition. Freeform optimization of microlens shape mainly changes $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$, which explains why it gains at most 7\% in any band over an equally optimized hemisphere on an extended OLED emitter.
