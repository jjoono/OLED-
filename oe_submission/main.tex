\title{Reciprocal Angular Transport Explains the Limited Benefit of Mixing in OLED Light Extraction}

% ---- authors: fill in ----
\author{Author One\authormark{1}, Author Two\authormark{1} and Author Three\authormark{1,*}}

\email{\authormark{*}[corresponding@email]}


\begin{abstract*}
We quantify how much angular mixing can improve light extraction from organic light-emitting diodes. The repeated interaction between an OLED and its extraction structure is described as angle-resolved transport constrained by optical reciprocity. With complete reciprocal mixing, recirculating light escapes with the étendue-weighted mean escape probability minus a term proportional to its angular variance. For 37 microlens, scattering, randomized-lens, and textured structures, complete mixing increases the spectrally weighted extraction by at most about two percentage points. A reduced model with one mixing parameter reproduces full transport calculations with a mean absolute error of 0.30 percentage points over 342 cases. Direct ray tracing and tests on structures outside the development set support the model, except for cone arrays.
\end{abstract*}


\section{Introduction}

A planar OLED loses much of its generated light to waveguided modes, electrode absorption, and total internal reflection at the substrate/air interface \cite{r1,r2}. External structures extract the substrate modes without modifying the electrical stack. Examples are microlens arrays (MLAs) \cite{r3,r4}, scattering layers \cite{r5,r6}, and textured films \cite{r7}. Their extraction efficiency has been mapped against lens profile, aspect ratio, and fill factor \cite{r3,r8}, and their shapes are increasingly optimized by inverse design and machine learning \cite{r9,r10}. With such structures, the highest reported external quantum efficiencies of single-unit OLEDs are 55–62\% \cite{r5,r11,r12,r13}. This limit has recently been attributed to the loss of returned light on each round trip through the device \cite{r14}.

However, no compact method exists for comparing the structures themselves. Statistical ray optics assumes that light in the substrate is fully randomized on every pass \cite{r15,r16,r17}. In this fully randomized model, the substrate-to-air efficiency $\eta = p/[p+(1-p)A']$ depends only on the escape probability $p$ and the round-trip loss $A'$ \cite{r14}. Angle-resolved models propagate the power distribution from pass to pass with redistribution matrices \cite{r2,r14,r18,r19,r20,r21,r22}. These models are accurate, but they are numerical and do not separate escape from redistribution. Where the degree of randomization has been quantified, it has been quantified empirically \cite{r23,r24,r25}. This difference is important. Because a hemispherical MLA returns unextracted light largely at its original angle \cite{r14}, the light reaching it on later passes is concentrated at angles that it transmits poorly. The fully randomized model therefore overestimates extraction, by an amount that depends on the structure. It is therefore not known how much improved angular mixing can increase extraction.

We address this question by analyzing the eigenvalues of the return matrix. Reciprocity makes this matrix self-adjoint in the étendue inner product. Within a rank-one return model, reciprocity also determines the angular redistribution of returned light uniquely. The light redistributed into each angle is proportional to the fraction that this angle returns. As a result, completely mixed light escapes with probability $p - \sigma^2/(1-p)$ instead of $p$, and the possible gain from mixing is limited by the angular variance $\sigma^2$ of the escape probability.

The second eigenvalue defines an angular mixing number. This number is the per-pass analogue of the transport factor $1/(1-g)$ in radiative transfer \cite{r26} and corresponds to the relaxation time of a reversible Markov chain \cite{r27}. The leading eigenvalue determines the escape probability of the quasi-stationary distribution \cite{r28}, which corresponds to the escape rate of open dynamical systems \cite{r29,r30,r31}. These quantities, and the reciprocity that makes them real and bounded, are standard in Markov-chain, open-system, and light-transport theory \cite{r32,r33,r34}. Reciprocal coupling of a diffuse lobe to angle-dependent reflection is likewise standard in reflectance modelling \cite{r35,r36}. Here we apply these concepts to passive light extraction and obtain three results. First, we derive a closed-form reference for the efficiency gain from complete reciprocal mixing. Second, we construct a reciprocal reduced model and test it against direct ray tracing and against structures not used in its development, including cases in which it fails. Third, we place OLED outcoupling structures on a plane of escape probability and mixing number, relate this plane to the radiance-ordered extraction bound \cite{r37,r38,r39}, and test it on a controlled comparison of freeform and hemispherical microlenses \cite{r11,r12}.

\section{Theory}

\subsection{Multi-pass extraction as a Markov chain on angle}

The substrate angle is divided into 1\ensuremath{^\circ} polar bins $k$. The quantity $v_k$ is the azimuth-integrated power travelling towards the structure in bin $k$, and $w_k = \sin^2\theta_{k+1}-\sin^2\theta_k$ is the étendue of the bin, so that uniform radiance corresponds to $v_k \propto w_k$. At each encounter with the structure, a fraction $p_k$ of the light in bin $k$ escapes, and a fraction $B_R(i\to r)$ returns from bin $i$ to bin $r$. The return matrix is therefore

\begin{equation}
B_R = M\,\mathrm{diag}(1-\mathbf{p}),
\end{equation}

where the column-stochastic matrix $M$ gives the distribution of the returned angle for light that is returned. $M$ is thus the transition matrix of a Markov chain on angle. The escape probability for uniform radiance is $p = \sum_k w_k p_k/\sum_k w_k$. The device reflects the returned light with its angle-resolved reflectance $R(\theta)$, and the substrate-to-air extraction efficiency is

\begin{equation}
\eta_{\mathrm{sta}} = \mathbf{p}^{\mathsf T}\left(I - \mathrm{diag}\,\mathbf{R}\; B_R\right)^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{(0)} ,
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{v}^{(0)}$ is the power launched into the substrate \cite{r14}. All losses enter through $R(\theta)$. The scalar $A' = 1-\sum_k w_k R_k/\sum_k w_k$ is used only for comparison with the fully randomized model.

The model is scalar and averages over azimuth and impact position. This approximation is accurate when correlations of these variables between successive encounters decay quickly. Direct $(\theta,\phi)$ tracing agrees with the model within 0.6 pp (Sec. 4.2), and treating s and p polarization as separate channels changes the conclusions by at most 0.4 pp (Table S18). Note S10 gives the estimators of $p_k$, $B_R$, and $M$ as pseudocode.

\subsection{Reciprocity and the angular mixing number}

For a passive, reciprocal structure \cite{r40},

\begin{equation}
w_i B_R(i\to j) = w_j B_R(j\to i).
\end{equation}

This relation has been noted for the redistribution matrices of textured sheets \cite{r19} and is the discrete form of the self-adjointness of reciprocal scattering operators in the étendue inner product \cite{r32}. $M$ is therefore similar to a symmetric matrix and has a real spectrum \cite{r33}. On the full angular domain, its stationary distribution is $\propto w(1-p)$. For the truncated chain used below, the stationary distribution is $\propto w s$, where $s_i$ is the fraction returned into the retained bins (Note S1). The eigenvalue of second-largest modulus, $\lambda_2$, determines how quickly the angular distribution relaxes. We define

\begin{equation}
N_{\mathrm{mix}} = \frac{1}{1-|\lambda_2|}
\end{equation}

as the relaxation time of the angular distribution, expressed in encounters. $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ is infinite for a planar interface and equals 1 for a Lambertian scatterer. For all structures studied here, $\lambda_2$ is real and positive.

Near-grazing states carry almost no étendue but can retain their angle for many encounters. The spectral metrics are therefore evaluated for $\theta < 85\ensuremath{^\circ}$, which excludes 0.76\% of the étendue, whereas $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ is always computed over 0–90\ensuremath{^\circ}. The mean error of the reduced model depends weakly on the cutoff and is 0.32, 0.30, 0.40, and 0.55 pp at 80\ensuremath{^\circ}, 85\ensuremath{^\circ}, 88\ensuremath{^\circ}, and 90\ensuremath{^\circ}. The cutoff changes the absolute values of the metrics but largely preserves the ranking of the structures (rank correlation 0.90–0.99; Note S1).

\subsection{Reciprocal reduced model}

The reduced model keeps the $p(\theta)$ of the structure. Of the returned light, a fraction $1-m$ keeps its angle and a fraction $m$ is redistributed with a fixed distribution $q$:

\begin{equation}
B_R(\theta_i\to\theta_r) = \left[1-p(\theta_i)\right]\left[(1-m)\,\delta_{ir} + m\,q(\theta_r)\right].
\end{equation}

Detailed balance holds only if $q \propto w(1-p)$. In this case, the light redistributed into each angle is proportional to the fraction that this angle returns. Reciprocal reflectance models that couple a diffuse lobe to angle-dependent specular reflection have the same form \cite{r35,r36}. To our knowledge, this condition has not been imposed in multi-pass extraction models, which redistribute returned light isotropically \cite{r15,r16,r17,r41}. For a structure whose escape probability depends on angle, isotropic redistribution places part of the returned light in the escape cone, although the escape cone itself returns almost no light. The normalized kernel has eigenvalues 1 and $1-m$, so that $m = 1-\lambda_2$.

\subsection{Complete reciprocal mixing as a reference}

For $m = 1$, the reciprocal kernel has rank one, $B_R(i\to r) = (1-p_i)\,q_r$ with $q \propto w(1-p)$. The escape probability of the mixed light is then

\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{mix}} = \sum_r q_r p_r = p - \frac{\sigma_p^2}{1-p}, \qquad \sigma_p^2 = \frac{\sum_k w_k (p_k - p)^2}{\sum_k w_k}.
\end{equation}

This quantity is also the late-pass escape probability (Sec. 2.5). Consequently, $\xi = 1 - \sigma_p^2/[p(1-p)] < 1$ even for complete mixing unless $p(\theta)$ is constant. The multi-pass series can then be summed in closed form,

\begin{equation}
\eta_{\mathrm{mix}} = \mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{v}^{(0)} + \frac{(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{u})\,[(\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{p})\cdot\mathbf{v}^{(0)}]}{1-(\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{p})\cdot\mathbf{u}}, \qquad u_k = R_k\,q_k .
\end{equation}

For an angle-independent reflectance $1-A'$, this expression becomes $\eta_{\mathrm{mix}} = p_0 + (1-p_0)\,p_{\mathrm{mix}}(1-A')/[p_{\mathrm{mix}} + (1-p_{\mathrm{mix}})A']$, where $p_0$ is the first-pass escape probability. It reduces to the fully randomized model only when $p(\theta)$ is constant.

A refractive surface transmits light inside the critical cone and returns all light outside it, so $\sigma_p^2$ is large. For a planar interface, $p$ = 0.40 but $p_{\mathrm{mix}}$ = 0.06. The fully randomized model assumes that mixed light escapes with probability $p$. It therefore overestimates the gain from mixing by an amount determined by the angular variance of escape.

$\eta_{\mathrm{mix}}$ is a reference for reciprocal angular redistribution and is not a universal upper bound. Other reciprocal return operators with the same $p(\theta)$ can extract more light. Note S1 gives a two-state example in which a reciprocal kernel of higher rank exceeds $\eta_{\mathrm{mix}}$ by 5 pp.

\subsection{Late-pass escape ratio}

$\lambda_2$ describes how quickly the angular distribution relaxes, but not the distribution that the light finally reaches. Light that circulates many times approaches the leading eigenvector of $B_R$, the quasi-stationary distribution \cite{r28}. This light escapes with probability $p_\infty = 1-\rho(B_R)$ per encounter, which corresponds to the escape rate of an open system \cite{r29,r30,r31}. The self-adjointness of $B_R$, the Rayleigh quotient with the uniform-radiance vector, and the column sums \cite{r42} give

\begin{equation}
\min_\theta p(\theta) \le p_\infty \le \tilde p ,
\end{equation}

where $\tilde p$ is the probability that uniform-radiance light leaves the truncated chain by escape or by return beyond the cutoff ($\tilde p - p < 0.008$). We define the late-pass escape ratio $\xi = p_\infty/\tilde p$. $\xi$ equals 1 when long-circulating light escapes as easily as Lambertian light and approaches 0 when some angles neither escape nor mix. All structures satisfy these bounds numerically (Note S1). $p_\infty$ characterizes the structure alone, in the limit of lossless return by the device. In an OLED, the survival operator $\mathrm{diag}\,\mathbf{R}\,B_R$ also contains parasitic loss, but it ranks the structures in the same order (Spearman correlation 0.96).

\subsection{Band-resolved radiance-ordered bound}

In one encounter, the power that a passive structure can transmit into an output band is bounded by the power obtained when the étendue of the band is filled with the brightest internal radiance first \cite{r37,r38,r39}. For uniform radiance, this bound equals the étendue ratio, which is $1/n^2$ for the hemisphere \cite{r15}. We evaluate the bound for each air-side polar band with the actual first-pass radiance of the microcavity, filling the étendue of each band with the substrate bins of highest $v^{(0)}_k/w_k$ first. For the structures, the band power is accumulated through the traced transmission matrix into air bins (Note S1).

\section{Methods}

\textbf{Devices.} Dipole emission and stack reflectance are computed with the Chance–Prock–Silbey model \cite{r43} extended to birefringent layers \cite{r44,r45} and with transfer matrices. The calculations use measured optical constants and an incoherent substrate \cite{r46}. The Al platform of the benchmark is Al (100 nm) / B3PyMPM (61.3 nm) / TCTA:B3PyMPM:Ir(dmppy-ph)$_2$tmd (25 nm) / TCTA (10 nm) / TAPC (97.5 nm) / ITO (150 nm) / glass ($n = 1.51$), with a horizontal dipole ratio of 0.865. A second stack replaces Al by Ag, and a low-loss stack follows ref. \cite{r14}. At 550 nm, $A'$ is 0.167, 0.044, and 0.026 for the three stacks (Table S2, Fig. S1). Device optics are computed for each wavelength with dispersive optical constants. The structure matrices are independent of wavelength (ray optics, non-dispersive index 1.51). Spectrally weighted quantities cover 400–700 nm in 10 nm steps and are weighted with the emitter spectrum multiplied by $\eta_{\mathrm{sub}}(\lambda)$ (Note S10).

\textbf{Structures.} All structures are index-matched to the substrate. The 38 structures are: a planar interface; six non-absorbing Henyey–Greenstein \cite{r47} scattering slabs ($g$ = 0–0.9, $\tau^*$ = 0.25–4), combined with the exit face by the adding method \cite{r48}; the hemispherical and freeform hexagonal MLAs of the benchmark; 17 random MLAs on a 6 \ensuremath{\times} 6 supercell with independent lenslet profiles (spread $s$), heights, or positions and radii; and five Gaussian-correlated wavy surfaces with rms slope 0.2–1.5, which resemble the buckles used on OLEDs \cite{r49}. MLAs and textures are traced with a height-field Monte Carlo ray tracer. This tracer reproduces the LightTools return matrix of the hemispherical MLA (escape probability 0.4275 compared with 0.4274). The adding method agrees with a direct ray trace of the slab within 0.05 pp. Ray optics requires features much larger than the wavelength \cite{r50}. Full-wave simulations of microlenses converge to ray tracing above about 5 µm \cite{r8}, and because ray optics is scale invariant, the results apply to any feature size in this range. Ray noise, realization, ray count, bin width, domain size, and the residual reciprocity asymmetry (relative residual 0.013–0.15) change $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ by at most 0.24, $\xi$ by at most 0.025, and $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ by at most 0.34 pp (Tables S5, S6, Note S10). As independent checks, we trace the complete multi-pass process directly in $(\theta, \phi)$, and we test the model on nine structures that were not used in its development.

\textbf{Freeform benchmark.} An axisymmetric freeform MLA with 13 variables (spline profile, height, and ETL and HTL thicknesses) is compared with an equally optimized hemispherical MLA with 3 variables. Both are evaluated on the Al platform with LightTools ray tracing, which has been validated against fabricated devices \cite{r11,r12}. The hemisphere is contained in the freeform design space (Table S10). Total EQE and the EQE in four polar bands (0–20\ensuremath{^\circ}, 20–40\ensuremath{^\circ}, 40–60\ensuremath{^\circ}, and 60–80\ensuremath{^\circ}) are optimized separately by surrogate optimization and pattern search (MATLAB surrogateopt and patternsearch). Each optimum is re-evaluated three times at full fidelity. In a restart control, every search is repeated from the hemispherical optimum (Note S6).

\section{Results}

\subsection{Efficiency gain from complete reciprocal mixing}

Fig.~\ref{fig:2} shows how much complete reciprocal mixing would increase the efficiency of each structure at its own $p(\theta)$. When the escape probability depends strongly on angle, $p_{\mathrm{mix}}$ is well below $p$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:2}a). The ratio $p_{\mathrm{mix}}/p$ is 0.87–0.91 for hemispherical MLAs, 0.81–0.93 for freeform and random MLAs, 0.37–0.90 for wavy surfaces, and 0.14 for the planar interface.

At 550 nm, complete mixing changes $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ of the 37 non-planar structures by -0.4 to +1.8 pp in the Al device. The maximum increase is 2.0 pp in the Ag device and 0.4 pp in the low-loss device (Fig.~\ref{fig:2}b). With spectral weighting by the emission spectrum (peak 572 nm), the maximum increases are 1.2, 1.5, and 2.2 pp for the Al, Ag, and low-loss devices. The fully randomized model predicts larger gains, by 0.6–2.9 pp for lens arrays, by 17 pp for the shallowest wavy surface, and by 31 pp for the planar interface. For the planar interface, complete mixing increases $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ only from 0.38 to 0.49. Mixing does not increase transmission at angles where the structure does not transmit. Only a change of $p(\theta)$ can increase it.

The gain from mixing is larger when the cavity emits into angles that the structure transmits poorly. At 500 nm, the first-pass escape probability of the hemispherical MLA decreases to 0.36 in the Al device and 0.33 in the Ag device, compared with $p$ = 0.42. Under these conditions, complete mixing would increase $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ by 5.7–6.4 pp (Al) and 8.4–9.4 pp (Ag), and random lenses and wavy surfaces achieve most of this increase (Table S19). Mixing is therefore most useful when the first-pass angular distribution does not match $p(\theta)$.

Two wavy surfaces (rms slope 0.4 and 0.7) exceed $\eta_{\mathrm{mix}}$ by 0.42 and 0.01 pp. They are the only structures whose escape probabilities at successive encounters are anticorrelated. In these structures, light that fails to escape is returned preferentially to angles with a high escape probability (Note S10).

\subsection{Validation of the reduced model}

Fig.~\ref{fig:3} compares four models with the full multi-pass series for 342 cases (three devices, three wavelengths, and 38 structures). The fully randomized model overestimates $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ of the planar interface by up to 60 pp and that of the hemispherical MLA by 2–3 pp (Fig.~\ref{fig:3}a). The opposite assumption, that every ray keeps its angle, underestimates $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ of scattering layers (Fig.~\ref{fig:3}b). The reduced model with $m = 1-\lambda_2$ and isotropic redistribution fits the lens arrays but overestimates shallow wavy surfaces by up to 10.5 pp (Fig.~\ref{fig:3}c). These surfaces have a small $\lambda_2$, but they return trapped light mostly to other trapped angles. Reciprocal redistribution accounts for this behaviour without an additional parameter. It reduces the mean absolute error to 0.30 pp (mean signed error -0.24 pp) and the maximum error to 3.0 pp (Fig.~\ref{fig:3}d). The error is not concentrated in any device or wavelength (Fig.~\ref{fig:3}e, Table S4).

Direct ray tracing in $(\theta, \phi)$ agrees with the full series within 0.6 pp for eight structures (Note S9, Table S16). The largest differences disappear when the azimuth is randomized at each pass, so they result from the azimuthal correlation that the chain neglects. For test structures not used in developing the model, the mean error of the reduced model is 0.48 pp and the maximum error is 3.3 pp. These structures are spherical caps, paraboloids, Gaussian bumps, a cone–hemisphere mixture, and wavy surfaces with a ring spectrum. For two cone arrays, the error reaches 20 pp. A single relaxation rate is therefore not sufficient for every reciprocal structure. For 60\ensuremath{^\circ} cones, the slow eigenmodes ($\lambda_2$ = 0.985) contain only 0.4\% of the returned light, and a rate weighted by the actual returned light ($\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}}$ = 0.43) restores the accuracy (0.23 pp). For 45\ensuremath{^\circ} cones, near-normal light is retroreflected within a closed group of angles that contains 98\% of the light, so that several relaxation rates are required (Note S9). A single rate is accurate when the returned light occupies the slow eigenmodes. A large $\lambda_2$ combined with a small fraction of returned light in the slow eigenmodes indicates that the reduced model may fail.

\subsection{Escape probability and mixing number of the structures}

The structure classes occupy separate regions of the $(p, N_{\mathrm{mix}})$ plane (Fig.~\ref{fig:4}a). The planar interface has $p$ = 0.40 and $N_{\mathrm{mix}} = \infty$. Scattering layers relax within 1.1–2.3 encounters but have lower escape probabilities, $p$ = 0.19–0.39. Hemispherical MLAs have the highest escape probability, $p$ = 0.42, and $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ = 6–8. Freeform MLAs have similar escape probabilities, $p$ = 0.42–0.43, and $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ = 3–7.5.

Randomization lowers $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ without lowering $p$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:4}b, Table S3). Independent lenslet profiles reduce $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ from 7.0 to 1.9 \ensuremath{\pm} 0.1. Position jitter of 30\% of the pitch reduces it to 3.5 \ensuremath{\pm} 0.1, and a height spread of \ensuremath{\pm}60\% reduces it only to 4.1 \ensuremath{\pm} 0.2 (\ensuremath{\pm} over three realizations, Table S5). Wavy surfaces with rms slope 0.4–1.5 reach $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ = 1.1–1.2, which is as low as that of a scattering layer.

The late-pass escape ratio $\xi$ provides information that $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ does not contain (Fig.~\ref{fig:4}c). For lens arrays, $\xi$ decreases from 0.80 to 0.39 as $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ increases from 2 to 8. In these arrays, light that keeps its angle accumulates at the angles where the lenses transmit least. The shallowest wavy surface ($\xi$ = 0.35) and a thin isotropic scattering layer ($\xi$ = 0.54) lie below this trend at $N_{\mathrm{mix}} \approx 2$. These structures relax quickly, but they return trapped light mainly to other trapped angles. They also have the largest residual errors in Fig.~\ref{fig:3}d. This order is the same for all cutoffs from 80\ensuremath{^\circ} to 90\ensuremath{^\circ} (Note S10).

The $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ contours in Fig.~\ref{fig:4}a are nearly vertical. The efficiency is therefore determined mainly by $p$. For the $p(\theta)$ shapes of lenses, scattering layers, and wavy surfaces, the full range of $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ changes $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ only as much as a change of $p$ by 0.02–0.05 (Table S7). Randomizing the lenslet profiles reduces $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ from 7.0 to 1.9 but changes $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ by less than 1 pp for the Al device at 550 nm. The gain from faster mixing (+0.9 pp) is offset by the change of $p(\theta)$ (-2.2 pp; Table S8).

\subsection{Radiance bound and angular composition of the output}

In the first pass, the microcavity emits a strongly anisotropic radiance into the substrate (Fig. S1). For the Al platform at 550 nm, the radiance-ordered bound allows 0.085, 0.205, 0.230, and 0.156 of the substrate power to enter the 0–20\ensuremath{^\circ}, 20–40\ensuremath{^\circ}, 40–60\ensuremath{^\circ}, and 60–80\ensuremath{^\circ} bands. Each value is the maximum for its band alone. For the whole hemisphere, the bound is 0.540, compared with $1/n^2$ = 0.439 for uniform radiance (Fig.~\ref{fig:5}a). All passive structures remain well below this bound. MLAs reach 76–80\% of the bound for the hemisphere, wavy surfaces reach 77\%, the planar interface reaches 65\%, and scattering layers reach 35–68\%. The lenses transmit almost the same power as they would for uniform radiance (0.43 compared with 0.44). The anisotropy of the cavity emission is therefore not used.

In lossy devices, the angular composition of the output is nearly independent of the structure (Fig.~\ref{fig:5}b). The selectivity $S_{40\text{–}60} = P_{\mathrm{air}}(40\text{–}60\ensuremath{^\circ})/P_{\mathrm{air}}(0\text{–}90\ensuremath{^\circ})$ is 0.33–0.36 for the Al device at 550 nm, close to the Lambertian value of 0.337. It is 0.32–0.36 at 500 and 600 nm and for the Ag device (Table S9, Fig. S2), and the Monte Carlo uncertainty is below 0.003. In the low-loss device, the range widens to 0.27–0.37. When little light is lost per round trip, late passes dominate the output, and hemispherical MLAs retain the angle of this light for many encounters.

\subsection{Case study: freeform versus an equally optimized hemisphere}

The results above also apply to a controlled shape-optimization benchmark. \section{Discussion}

\textbf{Saturation of lens-shape optimization.} A hemispherical array already reaches an escape probability close to the uniform-radiance étendue limit $1/n^2$. Changes of the lens profile therefore cannot increase $p$ appreciably. They change $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ and the shape of $p(\theta)$, and the efficiency contours are flat along these directions. In principle, the anisotropic emission of the cavity would allow a higher band power. A surface of the same area cannot increase the étendue of a band, but a surface with binary angular transmission can reach the radiance-ordered bound \cite{r39}, and skewness conservation does not exclude this \cite{r51}. The rotationally symmetric lenses and textures studied here, however, fill each band from a broad range of internal angles. They therefore extract approximately the power expected for uniform radiance. Reaching the band-resolved bound thus appears to require angle-selective transmission in addition to freedom in the lens profile.

\textbf{Limited gain from angular mixing.} Optical reciprocity couples angular redistribution to escape. In a reciprocal rank-one mixing model, the light redistributed into each angle is proportional to the fraction of incident light that the structure returns at that angle. The escape probability after complete mixing is therefore reduced by the étendue-weighted variance of the escape probability. For this reason, faster angular mixing does not necessarily increase extraction. In the structures examined, faster mixing is often accompanied by a reshaped or lower escape probability. Examples are randomized lenses (Table S8) and scattering layers, whose extraction is highest at intermediate scattering strength \cite{r18,r21}. For these structures, improving the escape profile is generally more effective than increasing the mixing rate.

\textbf{Design implications.} We suggest reporting three quantities for a new outcoupling structure. The escape probability $p$ determines the efficiency. $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ indicates how accurately the fully randomized model describes the structure. $\xi$ indicates whether light that circulates for many encounters can still be extracted. Random MLAs and wavy surfaces with rms slope of one or more combine high $p$, low $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$, and high $\xi$. Their spectrally weighted advantage over periodic MLAs is 0.2–0.5 pp in the Al and Ag devices and 2 pp in the low-loss device. The advantage is larger only at wavelengths where the first-pass angular distribution does not match $p(\theta)$. Irregular microlenses have been used on OLEDs mainly to avoid diffraction and colour shift \cite{r52}. Optimizers, including machine-learning methods \cite{r9,r10}, can be evaluated against the $(p, N_{\mathrm{mix}})$ plane and the band-resolved bound.

\textbf{Scope.} The analysis applies to passive external structures index-matched to the substrate in the ray-optical regime. Angle-selective filters, internal structures, absorbing scatterers, and wavelength converters are not included. As in earlier substrate models \cite{r2,r18}, the device is treated as an angle-preserving reflector. Diffuse reflection by the device would add a non-diagonal factor, and the product of this factor with $B_R$ does not necessarily have a real spectrum \cite{r33}.

\section{Conclusion}

We have quantified how much improved angular mixing can increase the outcoupling efficiency of passive structures. Reciprocity constrains angular redistribution. In a reciprocal return model with complete mixing, recirculating light escapes with probability $p - \sigma_p^2/(1-p)$. For 37 scattering, microlens, random-lens, and wavy structures, complete mixing therefore increases the spectrally weighted extraction by at most about 2 pp, whereas the fully randomized model predicts up to 19 pp. Larger gains occur only at wavelengths where the first-pass angular distribution does not match $p(\theta)$. This reference is not a universal upper bound, but it explains the limited gain from faster mixing in common passive structures. A reduced model based on the same reciprocity condition predicts multi-pass extraction with a mean absolute error of 0.30 pp and agrees with direct ray tracing. It predicts seven of nine structure types not used in its development and fails for cone arrays, for which a single relaxation rate does not describe the mixing. Lens-shape optimization mainly changes $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ and the angular composition of the output, consistent with gains of a few percent at most. Further improvement will likely require angle-selective structures.
