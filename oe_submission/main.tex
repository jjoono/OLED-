\title{Reciprocity Separates Reflective and Transmissive Angular Mixing in OLED Light Extraction}

% ---- authors: fill in ----
\author{Author One\authormark{1}, Author Two\authormark{1} and Author Three\authormark{1,*}}

\email{\authormark{*}[corresponding@email]}


\begin{abstract*}
Angular mixing is often assumed to help extract the light trapped in the substrate of organic light-emitting diodes, as in the fully randomized model of statistical ray optics. We show that optical reciprocity makes the benefit depend on where the light is mixed. Light mixed in reflection by the escape interface is redistributed in proportion to the fraction that each angle returns and escapes with $p - \sigma_p^2/(1-p)$, where $p$ is the étendue-weighted escape probability and $\sigma_p^2$ its angular variance. Light mixed in transmission through a lossless volume escapes with $p$, which recovers the fully randomized model, and a forward-scattering volume recovers 92–99\% of the gain of this limit over a planar interface. For 37 microlens, scattering, randomized-lens and textured structures, complete mixing by the structure raises the spectrally weighted extraction by at most about 2 percentage points (pp), and the distinction persists in slabs with refractive indices up to 3.5. A reduced model based on the same condition reproduces 342 full calculations with a mean error of 0.30 pp; a prospective test confirms that it fails when the angular chain contains closed sets. Microlens devices with two emitters show the predicted angular factors without adjustment and reveal that on-axis metrics overstate the gain of small lens patches.
\end{abstract*}


\section{Introduction}

A planar OLED loses much of its generated light to waveguided modes, electrode absorption, and total internal reflection at the substrate/air interface \cite{r1,r2}. External structures extract the substrate modes without modifying the electrical stack. Examples are microlens arrays (MLAs) \cite{r3,r4}, scattering layers \cite{r5,r6}, and textured films \cite{r7}. Their extraction efficiency has been mapped against lens profile, aspect ratio, and fill factor \cite{r3,r8}, and their shapes are increasingly optimized by inverse design and machine learning \cite{r9,r10}. With such structures, the highest reported external quantum efficiencies of single-unit OLEDs are 55–62\% \cite{r5,r11,r12,r13}. This limit has recently been attributed to the loss of returned light on each round trip through the device \cite{r14}.

However, no compact method exists for comparing the structures themselves. Statistical ray optics assumes that light in the substrate is fully randomized on every pass \cite{r15,r16,r17}. The substrate-to-air efficiency $\eta = p/[p+(1-p)A']$ then depends only on the escape probability $p$ and the round-trip loss $A'$ \cite{r14}. Angle-resolved models propagate the power distribution from pass to pass with redistribution matrices \cite{r2,r14,r18,r19,r20,r21,r22}. They are accurate but numerical, and the degree of randomization has been quantified only empirically \cite{r23,r24,r25}. Because a hemispherical MLA returns unextracted light largely at its original angle \cite{r14}, light on later passes is concentrated at angles that it transmits poorly. The fully randomized model therefore overestimates extraction by a structure-dependent amount, and it is not known how much improved angular mixing can increase extraction.

We show that the answer depends on where the light is mixed and that optical reciprocity determines this dependence. Reciprocity makes the return matrix self-adjoint in the étendue inner product. When the escape interface itself mixes the light in reflection, reciprocity fixes the redistribution: the light redistributed into each angle is proportional to the fraction that this angle returns. Completely mixed light then escapes with probability $p - \sigma_p^2/(1-p)$ instead of $p$, where $\sigma_p^2$ is the angular variance of the escape probability. Mixing in transmission through a lossless volume carries no such penalty, and mixing by the device is penalized only through the covariance of its reflectance with the escape probability. The fully randomized model therefore describes transmissive mixing, not the reflective mixing that refractive outcoupling structures provide.

We develop the consequences in four steps. First, we derive the escape probability of completely mixed light for the three placements and compare them for 38 structures and for slabs with exit media up to $n$ = 3.5 (Secs. 2.4, 2.5, 4.1, 4.2). Second, the same reciprocity condition gives a reduced model with one mixing parameter. Its second eigenvalue defines an angular mixing number, the per-pass analogue of the transport factor $1/(1-g)$ in radiative transfer \cite{r26} and the relaxation time of a reversible Markov chain \cite{r27}. We test the model against full transport, direct ray tracing and structures not used in its development, and we state when it fails (Sec. 4.3). Third, the leading eigenvalue gives the escape probability of the quasi-stationary distribution \cite{r28}, the escape rate of open dynamical systems \cite{r29,r30,r31}. These tools are standard in light-transport and reflectance theory \cite{r32,r33,r34,r35,r36}. Together with $p$ and the mixing number, it places all structures on one map, which we relate to the radiance-ordered bound \cite{r37,r38,r39} (Sec. 4.4). Fourth, we test the finite-geometry form of the model on fabricated devices with two emitters (Sec. 4.5) and summarize the robustness of the results and the design rules that follow from them (Secs. 4.6, 5).

\section{Theory}

\subsection{Multi-pass extraction as a Markov chain on angle}

The substrate angle is divided into 1\ensuremath{^\circ} polar bins $k$. The quantity $v_k$ is the azimuth-integrated power travelling towards the structure in bin $k$, and $w_k = \sin^2\theta_{k+1}-\sin^2\theta_k$ is the étendue of the bin, so that uniform radiance corresponds to $v_k \propto w_k$. At each encounter with the structure, a fraction $p_k$ of the light in bin $k$ escapes, and a fraction $B_R(i\to r)$ returns from bin $i$ to bin $r$. The return matrix is therefore

\begin{equation}
B_R = M\,\mathrm{diag}(1-\mathbf{p}),
\end{equation}

where the column-stochastic matrix $M$ gives the distribution of the returned angle for light that is returned. $M$ is thus the transition matrix of a Markov chain on angle. The escape probability for uniform radiance is $p = \sum_k w_k p_k/\sum_k w_k$. The device reflects the returned light with its angle-resolved reflectance $R(\theta)$, and the substrate-to-air extraction efficiency is

\begin{equation}
\eta_{\mathrm{sta}} = \mathbf{p}^{\mathsf T}\left(I - \mathrm{diag}\,\mathbf{R}\; B_R\right)^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{(0)} ,
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{v}^{(0)}$ is the power launched into the substrate \cite{r14}. All losses enter through $R(\theta)$. The scalar $A' = 1-\sum_k w_k R_k/\sum_k w_k$ is used only for comparison with the fully randomized model.

The model is scalar and averages over azimuth and impact position. Direct $(\theta,\phi)$ tracing agrees with it within 0.6 pp (Sec. 4.3), and separate s and p channels change the conclusions by at most 0.4 pp (Table S7). Note S10 gives the estimators as pseudocode.

\subsection{Reciprocity and the angular mixing number}

For a passive, reciprocal structure \cite{r40},

\begin{equation}
w_i B_R(i\to j) = w_j B_R(j\to i).
\end{equation}

This relation has been noted for the redistribution matrices of textured sheets \cite{r19} and is the discrete form of the self-adjointness of reciprocal scattering operators in the étendue inner product \cite{r32}. $M$ is therefore similar to a symmetric matrix and has a real spectrum \cite{r33}. On the full angular domain, its stationary distribution is $\propto w(1-p)$. For the truncated chain used below, the stationary distribution is $\propto w s$, where $s_i$ is the fraction returned into the retained bins (Note S1). The eigenvalue of second-largest modulus, $\lambda_2$, determines how quickly the angular distribution relaxes. We define

\begin{equation}
N_{\mathrm{mix}} = \frac{1}{1-|\lambda_2|}
\end{equation}

as the relaxation time of the angular distribution, expressed in encounters. $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ is infinite for a planar interface and equals 1 for a Lambertian scatterer. For all structures studied here, $\lambda_2$ is real and positive.

Near-grazing states carry almost no étendue but can retain their angle for many encounters. The spectral metrics are therefore evaluated for $\theta < 85\ensuremath{^\circ}$ (0.76\% of the étendue is excluded), whereas $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ is always computed over 0–90\ensuremath{^\circ}. Cutoffs between 80\ensuremath{^\circ} and 90\ensuremath{^\circ} change the mean error by less than 0.3 pp (Note S1).

\subsection{Reciprocal reduced model}

The reduced model keeps the $p(\theta)$ of the structure. Of the returned light, a fraction $1-m$ keeps its angle and a fraction $m$ is redistributed with a fixed distribution $q$:

\begin{equation}
B_R(\theta_i\to\theta_r) = \left[1-p(\theta_i)\right]\left[(1-m)\,\delta_{ir} + m\,q(\theta_r)\right].
\end{equation}

Detailed balance holds only if $q \propto w(1-p)$. In this case, the light redistributed into each angle is proportional to the fraction that this angle returns. Reciprocal reflectance models that couple a diffuse lobe to angle-dependent specular reflection use the same rank-one kernel \cite{r35,r36}. To our knowledge, this condition has not been imposed in multi-pass extraction models, which assume isotropic internal radiance or redistribute returned light isotropically \cite{r15,r16,r17,r41}. What is new here is therefore not the kernel but its consequence for extraction: the classification of mixing by its placement (Sec. 2.5) and the identification of the fully randomized model with transmissive mixing. For a structure whose escape probability depends on angle, isotropic redistribution places part of the returned light in the escape cone, although the escape cone itself returns almost no light. The normalized kernel has eigenvalues 1 and $1-m$, so that $m = 1-\lambda_2$.

\subsection{Complete mixing at the escape interface}

For $m = 1$, the reciprocal kernel has rank one, $B_R(i\to r) = (1-p_i)\,q_r$ with $q \propto w(1-p)$. The escape probability of the mixed light is then

\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{mix}} = \sum_r q_r p_r = p - \frac{\sigma_p^2}{1-p}, \qquad \sigma_p^2 = \frac{\sum_k w_k (p_k - p)^2}{\sum_k w_k}.
\end{equation}

This quantity is also the late-pass escape probability (Sec. 2.6). Consequently, the late-pass escape ratio defined in Sec. 2.6 is $\xi = 1 - \sigma_p^2/[p(1-p)] < 1$ even for complete mixing unless $p(\theta)$ is constant. The multi-pass series can then be summed in closed form,

\begin{equation}
\eta_{\mathrm{mix}} = \mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{v}^{(0)} + \frac{(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{u})\,[(\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{p})\cdot\mathbf{v}^{(0)}]}{1-(\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{p})\cdot\mathbf{u}}, \qquad u_k = R_k\,q_k .
\end{equation}

For an angle-independent reflectance $1-A'$, this expression becomes $\eta_{\mathrm{mix}} = p_0 + (1-p_0)\,p_{\mathrm{mix}}(1-A')/[p_{\mathrm{mix}} + (1-p_{\mathrm{mix}})A']$, where $p_0$ is the first-pass escape probability. It reduces to the fully randomized model only when $p(\theta)$ is constant.

A refractive surface transmits light inside the critical cone and returns all light outside it, so $\sigma_p^2$ is large. For a planar interface, $p$ = 0.40 but $p_{\mathrm{mix}}$ = 0.06. The fully randomized model assumes that mixed light escapes with probability $p$. It therefore overestimates the gain from mixing by an amount determined by the angular variance of escape.

$\eta_{\mathrm{mix}}$ is a reference for reciprocal angular redistribution and is not a universal upper bound. Other reciprocal return operators with the same $p(\theta)$ can extract more light. Note S1 gives a two-state example in which a reciprocal kernel of higher rank exceeds $\eta_{\mathrm{mix}}$ by 5 pp.

\subsection{Mixing in transmission and by the device}

The reduction derived in Sec. 2.4 arises because the escape interface itself redistributes the returned light. Reciprocity weights redistribution in reflection by the return probability of the reflecting element, which is $1-p$ for the escape interface. Two other ways of mixing the light behave differently.

First, the light can be mixed in transmission by a lossless layer with negligible reflection, such as an ideal volume randomizer between the device and the escape interface. Each pass through such a layer distributes the light in proportion to $w$, independently of $p$ and $R$. The mixed light therefore escapes with probability $p$, and complete mixing of this kind reproduces the fully randomized model exactly. In a lossless, index-matched substrate, the position of the layer between the device and the escape interface does not affect the result (Note S11). Real scattering layers also reflect part of the light back towards the device, which lowers their own escape probability (Sec. 4.4).

Second, the device can mix the light in reflection, for example at a diffusely reflecting electrode. The structure then keeps its own return matrix $B_R$, and the device reflection becomes a redistribution kernel $D$. Reciprocity of the device requires $w_i D(i\to j) = w_j D(j\to i)$, and the column sums of $D$ equal the reflectance $R_i$. The rank-one kernel that satisfies both conditions is

\begin{equation}
D(i\to j) = \frac{R_i\,R_j\,w_j}{\sum_k w_k R_k} .
\end{equation}

Light mixed by the device is therefore distributed in proportion to $wR$, and its escape probability at the next encounter is

\begin{equation}
p_D = \frac{\sum_k w_k R_k\,p_k}{\sum_k w_k R_k} = p + \frac{\mathrm{Cov}_w(R,p)}{\langle R\rangle_w},
\end{equation}

where the covariance and the mean are étendue weighted. The variance term of Sec. 2.4 does not appear. If $R$ does not depend on angle, $p_D = p$. When the device absorbs most strongly inside the escape cone, as in strong microcavities, the covariance is negative and mixing by the device is also reduced (Note S11).

Table~\ref{tab:1} summarizes the three placements. Only reflective mixing at the escape interface weights the redistribution by the probability of not escaping, and only this placement carries the variance penalty.

\subsection{Late-pass escape ratio}

$\lambda_2$ describes how quickly the angular distribution relaxes, but not the distribution that the light finally reaches. Light that circulates many times approaches the leading eigenvector of $B_R$, the quasi-stationary distribution \cite{r28}. This light escapes with probability $p_\infty = 1-\rho(B_R)$ per encounter, which corresponds to the escape rate of an open system \cite{r29,r30,r31}. The self-adjointness of $B_R$, the Rayleigh quotient with the uniform-radiance vector, and the column sums \cite{r42} give

\begin{equation}
\min_\theta p(\theta) \le p_\infty \le \tilde p ,
\end{equation}

where $\tilde p$ is the probability that uniform-radiance light leaves the truncated chain by escape or by return beyond the cutoff ($\tilde p - p < 0.008$). We define the late-pass escape ratio $\xi = p_\infty/\tilde p$. $\xi$ equals 1 when long-circulating light escapes as easily as Lambertian light and approaches 0 when some angles neither escape nor mix. All structures satisfy these bounds numerically (Note S1). $p_\infty$ characterizes the structure alone, in the limit of lossless return by the device. In an OLED, the survival operator $\mathrm{diag}\,\mathbf{R}\,B_R$ also contains parasitic loss, but it ranks the structures in the same order (Spearman correlation 0.96).

\section{Methods}

\textbf{Devices.} Dipole emission and stack reflectance are computed with the Chance–Prock–Silbey model \cite{r43} extended to birefringent layers \cite{r44,r45} and with transfer matrices. The calculations use measured optical constants and an incoherent substrate \cite{r46}. The Al platform of the benchmark is Al (100 nm) / B3PyMPM (61.3 nm) / TCTA:B3PyMPM:Ir(dmppy-ph)$_2$tmd (25 nm) / TCTA (10 nm) / TAPC (97.5 nm) / ITO (150 nm) / glass ($n = 1.51$), with a horizontal dipole ratio of 0.865. A second stack replaces Al by Ag, and a low-loss stack follows ref. \cite{r14}. At 550 nm, $A'$ is 0.167, 0.044, and 0.026 for the three stacks (Table S2, Fig. S1). Device optics are computed for each wavelength with dispersive optical constants. The structure matrices are independent of wavelength (ray optics, non-dispersive index 1.51). Spectrally weighted quantities cover 400–700 nm in 10 nm steps and are weighted with the emitter spectrum multiplied by $\eta_{\mathrm{sub}}(\lambda)$ (Note S10).

\textbf{Structures.} All structures are index-matched to the substrate. The 38 structures, of which 37 are non-planar, are: a planar interface; six non-absorbing Henyey–Greenstein \cite{r47} scattering slabs ($g$ = 0–0.9, $\tau^*$ = 0.25–4), combined with the exit face by the adding method \cite{r48}; nine hexagonal MLAs from the benchmark (hemispherical and freeform optima for total EQE and for the angular bands); 17 random MLAs on a 6 \ensuremath{\times} 6 supercell with independent lenslet profiles (spread $s$), heights, or positions and radii; and five Gaussian-correlated wavy surfaces with rms slope 0.2–1.5, which resemble the buckles used on OLEDs \cite{r49}. MLAs and textures are traced with a height-field Monte Carlo ray tracer that reproduces the LightTools return matrix of the hemispherical MLA (escape probability 0.4275 compared with 0.4274). Ray optics applies to features much larger than the wavelength \cite{r50}, which for microlenses means above about 5 µm \cite{r8}. Numerical parameters and the residual reciprocity asymmetry change $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ by at most 0.24, $\xi$ by at most 0.025, and $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ by at most 0.34 pp (Tables S5, S6, Note S10).

\textbf{Fabricated devices and finite geometry.} The fabricated devices (Sec. 4.5) are modelled with the chain extended to lateral position. Between encounters with the front face, light moves laterally by $2t\tan\theta$. The back-face reflectance depends on the region of the substrate layout (pixel, Al-covered, uncovered, with or without ITO), and light that reaches the blackened edges is lost. The lens kernels are traced with the orientation of the lattice resolved, and the photodiode goniometer is modelled with its scan plane, rotation axis and detector size. In the limit of an infinite substrate, this estimator reproduces $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ to $2\times10^{-4}$ (Note S13).

\section{Results}

\subsection{Where the light is mixed}

Fig.~\ref{fig:2}b compares complete mixing in reflection at the escape interface with complete mixing in reflection at the device (Al, 550 nm). The difference between the two efficiencies increases with $p - p_{\mathrm{mix}} = \sigma_p^2/(1-p)$ (correlation coefficient 0.97). For the planar interface, $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ is 0.384 without mixing, 0.488 with mixing at the escape interface, 0.770 with mixing at the device, and 0.798 with a lossless transmissive randomizer, which equals the fully randomized model. For lens arrays, where $\sigma_p^2$ is small, mixing by the device increases $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ by 1.5–4.1 pp, compared with at most 1.8 pp at the escape interface. Mixing by the device comes within 2.2 pp of the fully randomized model for all non-planar structures in the Al device and within 1.2 pp in the Ag and low-loss devices, because their reflectance is nearly uncorrelated with $p(\theta)$ ($|p_D - p| < 0.008$, Note S11). With spectral weighting, mixing by the device adds 1.1–3.3 pp for lens arrays in the Al device, compared with at most 0.6 pp at the escape interface (Table S29).

Transmissive mixing does not require an ideal randomizer. A non-absorbing Henyey–Greenstein volume placed under the planar exit face mixes the light in transmission but also reflects part of it (Fig.~\ref{fig:2}d). For forward scattering ($g$ = 0.8–0.9) at $\tau^\emph{$ ≈ 0.5, it reaches $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ = 0.764 in the Al device, 92\% of the way from the planar interface (0.384) to the ideal transmissive value (0.798), compared with 0.488 for complete reflective mixing by the interface; in the Ag and low-loss devices it reaches 97\% and 99\%. Thicker volumes reflect more of the light and fall back to 0.58 at $\tau^}$ = 4. A real volume scatterer can therefore approach the transmissive limit, whereas a refractive structure that mixes through its own escape interface cannot.

The same distinction applies to any slab with an exit face and a back reflector (Fig.~\ref{fig:2}c). We consider a slab with an internal isotropic source and an angle-independent back reflectance $R$ = 0.90, for which complete mixing at the back reflector equals complete transmissive mixing. For a planar exit face, the extraction efficiency is 0.44 without mixing, 0.60 with complete mixing at the exit face, and 0.87 with complete mixing at the back reflector at $n$ = 1.5, and 0.08, 0.19, and 0.37 at $n$ = 3.5. A textured exit face transmits a similar fraction from every internal angle, so its $\sigma_p^2$ is small (Table S28). Hemispherical-MLA and steep wavy faces therefore come within 1.1 pp of the fully randomized result at $n \ge 2.4$, and complete mixing at the exit face changes the efficiency by at most 2.0 pp. A shallow wavy face (rms slope 0.4) stays up to 5.8 pp below this result. Mixing at its exit face recovers none of the difference, whereas mixing at the back reflector recovers all of it.

\subsection{Gain from complete mixing by the structure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:2} shows how much complete reciprocal mixing by the structure itself would increase the efficiency of each structure at its own $p(\theta)$. When the escape probability depends strongly on angle, $p_{\mathrm{mix}}$ is well below $p$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:2}a): $p_{\mathrm{mix}}/p$ is 0.81–0.93 for lens arrays, 0.37–0.90 for wavy surfaces, and 0.14 for the planar interface.

At 550 nm, complete mixing changes $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ of the 37 non-planar structures by -0.4 to +1.8 pp in the Al device. The maximum increase is 2.0 pp in the Ag device and 0.4 pp in the low-loss device (Fig.~\ref{fig:2}e). With spectral weighting by the emission spectrum (peak 572 nm), the maximum increases are 1.1, 1.4, and 2.2 pp for the Al, Ag, and low-loss devices. The fully randomized model predicts larger gains, by 0.6–2.9 pp for lens arrays, by 17 pp for the shallowest wavy surface, and by 31 pp for the planar interface. Mixing cannot raise transmission at angles where the structure does not transmit.

The gain from mixing is larger when the cavity emits into angles that the structure transmits poorly. At 500 nm, the first-pass escape probability of the hemispherical MLA decreases to 0.36 in the Al device and 0.33 in the Ag device, compared with $p$ = 0.42. Under these conditions, complete mixing would increase $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ by 5.7–6.4 pp (Al) and 8.4–9.4 pp (Ag), and random lenses and wavy surfaces achieve most of this increase (Table S10). Mixing is therefore most useful when the first-pass angular distribution does not match $p(\theta)$.

Two wavy surfaces (rms slope 0.4 and 0.7) exceed $\eta_{\mathrm{mix}}$ by 0.42 and 0.01 pp; the second difference is below the numerical uncertainty of 0.34 pp. Only in these structures are the escape probabilities at successive encounters anticorrelated (Note S10).

\subsection{Validation of the reduced model}

We compare four models with the full multi-pass series for 342 cases (three devices, three wavelengths, and 38 structures). The fully randomized model overestimates $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ of the planar interface by up to 60 pp (Ag device, 500 nm) and that of the hemispherical MLA by 2–3 pp (Fig. S2a). The opposite assumption, that every ray keeps its angle, underestimates $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ of scattering layers (Fig. S2b). The reduced model with $m = 1-\lambda_2$ and isotropic redistribution fits the lens arrays but overestimates shallow wavy surfaces by up to 10.5 pp (Fig. S2c). These surfaces have a small $\lambda_2$, but they return trapped light mostly to other trapped angles. Reciprocal redistribution accounts for this behaviour without an additional parameter. It reduces the mean absolute error to 0.30 pp (mean signed error -0.24 pp) and the maximum error to 3.0 pp (Fig.~\ref{fig:3}a). The error is not concentrated in any device or wavelength (Fig.~\ref{fig:3}b, Table S4).

Direct ray tracing in $(\theta, \phi)$ agrees with the full series within 0.6 pp for eight structures (Note S9, Table S19). The remaining differences disappear when the azimuth is randomized at each pass, so they result from the azimuthal correlation that the chain neglects. For test structures not used in developing the model (spherical caps, paraboloids, Gaussian bumps, a cone–hemisphere mixture, and wavy surfaces with a ring spectrum), the mean error is 0.48 pp and the maximum error is 3.3 pp. For two cone arrays, the error reaches 20 pp.

These two failures suggested two conditions for a single rate $1-\lambda_2$ to describe the light that actually circulates (Fig.~\ref{fig:3}c), both of which can be evaluated from $M$ and the first-pass distribution without the multi-pass series. First, the chain must be irreducible: the 45\ensuremath{^\circ} cones retroreflect light within 0–4\ensuremath{^\circ} (0.5\% of the étendue) into itself, a closed set that gives $\lambda_2 = 1$ although the remaining angles mix. Second, the slowest mode must carry a significant share of the returned light, which we measure by the relaxation rate weighted by the returned light, $\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}}$ (Note S9). For the 60\ensuremath{^\circ} cones, $\lambda_2$ = 0.985 but $\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}}$ = 0.43. All 45 other structures satisfy both conditions ($\lambda_2 - \lambda_{\mathrm{eff}} \le 0.35$) and have a maximum error of 3.3 pp. Because these conditions were identified after the out-of-sample test, we tested them prospectively on twelve further structures (cones with side angles of 30–70\ensuremath{^\circ}, square and inverted pyramids, and one-dimensional prism gratings), recording the predictions before evaluating the multi-pass series (Note S9). The first condition held: the 45\ensuremath{^\circ} pyramids and 45\ensuremath{^\circ} prisms form closed sets, were predicted to fail, and have errors of 21 and 23 pp. The second condition did not discriminate: it flagged two structures that the model describes within 2.5 pp, and the eight structures that passed both conditions have a mean error of 1.4 pp but a maximum of 7.5 pp (40\ensuremath{^\circ} cones). For faceted structures, the reduced model is therefore less accurate than for the curved lenses, scatterers and wavy surfaces of the library, although its mean error remains below that of the fully randomized model (2.8 pp). The direct traces confirm the full series for the 60\ensuremath{^\circ} cones within 0.6 pp, so the failures are those of the one-rate reduction, not of the angle-resolved model.

\subsection{Escape probability and mixing number of the structures}

The structure classes occupy separate regions of the $(p, N_{\mathrm{mix}})$ plane (Fig.~\ref{fig:4}a). The planar interface has $p$ = 0.40 and $N_{\mathrm{mix}} = \infty$. Scattering layers relax within 1.1–2.3 encounters but have lower escape probabilities, $p$ = 0.19–0.39. Hemispherical MLAs have the highest escape probability, $p$ = 0.42, and $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ = 6–8. Freeform MLAs have similar escape probabilities, $p$ = 0.42–0.43, and $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ = 3–7.5.

Randomization lowers $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ without lowering $p$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:4}b, Table S3). Independent lenslet profiles reduce $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ from 7.0 to 1.9, position jitter of 30\% of the pitch to 3.5, and a height spread of \ensuremath{\pm}60\% only to 4.1 (Table S5). Wavy surfaces with rms slope 0.4–1.5 reach $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ = 1.1–1.2, as low as a scattering layer.

The late-pass escape ratio $\xi$ provides information that $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ does not contain (Fig.~\ref{fig:4}c). For lens arrays, $\xi$ decreases from 0.80 to 0.39 as $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ increases from 2 to 8, because light that keeps its angle accumulates where the lenses transmit least. The shallowest wavy surface ($\xi$ = 0.35) and a thin isotropic scattering layer ($\xi$ = 0.54) lie below this trend at $N_{\mathrm{mix}} \approx 2$. They relax quickly but return trapped light mainly to other trapped angles, and they have the largest residual errors in Fig.~\ref{fig:3}a.

The $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ contours in Fig.~\ref{fig:4}a are nearly vertical. The efficiency is therefore determined mainly by $p$. For the $p(\theta)$ shapes of lenses, scattering layers, and wavy surfaces, the full range of $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ changes $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ only as much as a change of $p$ by 0.02–0.05 (Table S8). Randomizing the lenslet profiles reduces $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ from 7.0 to 1.9 but changes $\eta_{\mathrm{sta}}$ by less than 1 pp for the Al device at 550 nm. The gain from faster mixing (+0.9 pp) is offset by the change of $p(\theta)$ (-2.2 pp; Table S9).

\subsection{Experimental test on fabricated devices}

We tested the finite-geometry model on fabricated devices with two emitters (Fig.~\ref{fig:5}a). The orange devices are ITO (150 nm) / TAPC (50 nm) / TCTA (10 nm) / TCTA:B3PyMPM:Ir(dmppy-ph)$_2$tmd (25 nm) / B3PyMPM (45 nm) / LiF (1 nm) / Al (100 nm) on 0.7 mm glass, and the green devices contain Ir(ppy)$_2$acac. A pixel-defining layer opens a circle of 1 mm radius. For the lens devices, a second 0.7 mm glass with a hexagonal array of hemispherical or freeform lenslets (radius 10 µm) on a 3 \ensuremath{\times} 3 mm square centred on the pixel is coupled with index-matching oil. EQE and angular intensity were measured with a photodiode goniometer. The cavity and a common internal efficiency (0.89, orange; 0.95, green) are fixed by the bare devices of each emitter, and the lens devices are then predicted without further parameters (Note S13). Because the orientation of the hexagonal lattice in the scan plane was not recorded, the predictions are given as ranges over the lattice orientation, scan plane, rotation axis and photodiode size.

With this small patch, the lenses act mainly on first-pass light: in the 1.4 mm thick substrate, most light returned by the lenses lands outside the patch, where the planar face traps it until it reaches the blackened edges. The model predicts the resulting angular signature for both emitters without adjustment (Fig.~\ref{fig:5}b–d). The hemispherical array concentrates the emission towards the normal. Its angular factor, the EQE divided by the Lambertian EQE inferred from the on-axis intensity, is predicted as 0.62–0.68 (orange) and 0.65–0.71 (green) over the measurement geometries and measured as 0.66 \ensuremath{\pm} 0.04 (five devices) and 0.71 \ensuremath{\pm} 0.02 (one device, six scans). The freeform arrays spread first-pass light, with 0.92–1.04 and 0.83–0.91 predicted and 1.07 \ensuremath{\pm} 0.07 (four devices) and 0.87 \ensuremath{\pm} 0.04 (three devices) measured. For the bare devices, 0.99–1.02 and 1.00–1.02 are predicted and 1.01 \ensuremath{\pm} 0.05 (five devices) and 1.02 (two devices) measured. All device means lie within the predicted ranges or, for the orange freeform devices, 0.03 above them; individual orange devices scatter by up to 0.09 beyond the range. The normalized intensities of the hemispherical devices deviate from the best-matching geometry by 0.02–0.06 rms between 10\ensuremath{^\circ} and 80\ensuremath{^\circ}.

The experiment also shows that on-axis metrics overstate the effect of small lens patches (Fig.~\ref{fig:5}e). The hemispherical devices have 1.22 (orange) and 1.21 (green) times the on-axis EQE of the bare devices, but only 0.81 and 0.85 times their EQE. The model predicts the same contrast, 1.39–1.55 on axis compared with 0.94–1.01 in total. The absolute EQE of all lens devices is 84–91\% of the prediction, and the on-axis ratio is lower by a similar factor. A single angle-independent loss in the attached lens glass accounts for this: fitted to the orange hemispherical devices (a transmission of 0.925 per crossing of the oil-coupled interface, the only fitted parameter of the lens devices), it predicts 0.89, 0.87 and 0.90 of the lossless EQE for the orange freeform, green hemispherical and green freeform devices, compared with 0.90, 0.84 and 0.91 measured, and it changes the angular factors by at most 0.02 (Note S13). Lens-shape variants cannot account for the difference, because those that lower the predicted on-axis gain to the measured value raise the angular factor above its measured range (Table S35). For a 10 \ensuremath{\times} 10 mm patch, the model predicts 1.28–1.77 times the bare EQE. A lens array thus extracts recycled light only if it extends several substrate thicknesses beyond the pixel, and on-axis measurements of small patches can indicate a gain where the total extraction decreases.

Table~\ref{tab:2} collects the tests of the central result: reflective mixing by the escape interface adds little, whereas mixing in transmission or by an angle-independent reflector recovers the fully randomized model. The result holds for three devices, three wavelengths and spectral weighting, for exit media up to $n$ = 3.5, for a finite substrate with absorbing edges, and under the numerical, polarization and azimuthal checks. Reflective mixing becomes useful only when the first-pass distribution of the cavity does not match $p(\theta)$, as at 500 nm.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Why the placement of mixing matters.} Optical reciprocity couples angular redistribution to escape. Light mixed in reflection by the escape interface is redistributed in proportion to the fraction that each angle returns, so its escape probability is reduced by the variance of $p(\theta)$. The fully randomized model implicitly assumes the opposite weighting, which only transmissive mixing in a lossless volume provides. Faster mixing by a refractive structure therefore does not necessarily increase extraction, and in the structures examined it is often accompanied by a reshaped or lower escape probability. Examples are randomized lenses (Table S9) and scattering layers, whose extraction is highest at intermediate scattering strength \cite{r18,r21}. A scattering volume with little backscattering can approach the fully randomized model, whereas a refractive structure that mixes the light through its own escape interface cannot (Secs. 2.5 and 4.1).

\textbf{Saturation of lens-shape optimization.} A hemispherical array already reaches an escape probability close to the uniform-radiance étendue limit $1/n^2$, so changes of the lens profile mainly change $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ and the shape of $p(\theta)$, along which the efficiency contours are flat. The anisotropic first-pass emission of the cavity would in principle allow more: for the Al platform at 550 nm, the radiance-ordered bound is 0.540, compared with $1/n^2$ = 0.439 (Fig. S4, Note S4). The lenses, however, transmit almost the same power as for uniform radiance (0.43), because they fill each band from a broad range of internal angles. In a controlled LightTools benchmark of the device platform of \cite{r11,r12}, band-optimized freeform lenses exceed an equally optimized hemisphere by at most 7\% in one band and 1.3\% in total EQE (Note S12). A surface with binary angular transmission can reach the bound \cite{r39}, and skewness conservation does not exclude this \cite{r51}, so reaching it appears to require angle-selective transmission. The angular composition of the output is also nearly independent of the structure: the fraction in 40–60\ensuremath{^\circ} is 0.32–0.36, close to the Lambertian 0.337 (Fig. S3, Table S11).

\textbf{Design rules.} The results give six rules for outcoupling structures.

1. Estimate what mixing can add before optimizing it. Complete reflective mixing by a structure can raise the escape probability of recirculating light only to $p - \sigma_p^2/(1-p)$; for the lens arrays studied, $\sigma_p^2/(1-p)$ = 0.03–0.08, and the gain is at most about 2 pp.
2. Mix where reciprocity does not penalize it: in a weakly absorbing, forward-scattering volume ($g \ge 0.8$, $\tau^* \approx 0.5$ recovers 92–99\% of the transmissive gain over a planar interface, Fig.~\ref{fig:2}d), or at a device whose reflectance is uncorrelated with $p(\theta)$ ($|p_D - p| < 0.008$ for the stacks here). For lens arrays in the Al device, mixing by the device adds 1.5–4.1 pp.
3. Rank structures by $p$ first. $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ and $\xi$ matter mainly when the first-pass distribution of the cavity does not match $p(\theta)$, where mixing can add 5–9 pp. Random MLAs and wavy surfaces with rms slope of one or more combine high $p$, low $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ and high $\xi$, but their spectrally weighted advantage over periodic MLAs is only 0.2–0.5 pp in the Al and Ag devices, comparable to the numerical uncertainty of 0.34 pp, and 2 pp in the low-loss device; irregular microlenses have been used on OLEDs mainly to avoid diffraction and colour shift \cite{r52}.
4. Use the one-rate reduced model only for irreducible chains, and expect errors of several percentage points for faceted structures (Sec. 4.3); otherwise use the full matrix, which costs little more. Optimizers, including machine-learning methods \cite{r9,r10}, can be evaluated against the $(p, N_{\mathrm{mix}})$ plane.
5. Make the lens patch several substrate thicknesses wider than the pixel. For the 1.4 mm substrate of Sec. 4.5, a hemispherical patch of 3 mm lowers the EQE, about 6 mm gives half of the gain of a 20 mm patch, and 10 mm gives 80\% (Table S36).
6. Report the angle-integrated EQE. In the first-pass regime, the on-axis EQE overstates the effect of a lens patch by a factor of about 1.5.

\textbf{Scope.} The analysis applies to passive external structures index-matched to the substrate in the ray-optical regime. The extraction of waveguided modes by internal structures, and the change of the cavity that such structures cause, are not included. Angle-selective filters, absorbing scatterers, and wavelength converters are also not included. Except in Secs. 2.5 and 4.1, the device is treated as an angle-preserving reflector, as in earlier substrate models \cite{r2,r18}. For a partly diffuse electrode, the gain increases nearly linearly with the diffuse fraction (Table S30), but the product of the device kernel with $B_R$ need not have a real spectrum \cite{r33}. Lens patches only a few substrate thicknesses wide act mainly on first-pass light (Sec. 4.5). Light trapping in solar cells is the reciprocal problem, in which a low escape probability is desirable, and is not analysed here.

\section{Conclusion}

Optical reciprocity makes the benefit of angular mixing depend on where the light is mixed. Light mixed in reflection by the escape interface escapes with probability $p - \sigma_p^2/(1-p)$, light mixed in transmission through a lossless volume with $p$, and light mixed by the device with $p + \mathrm{Cov}_w(R,p)/\langle R\rangle_w$. The fully randomized model of statistical ray optics therefore describes transmissive mixing, not the reflective mixing that refractive outcoupling structures provide. For 37 scattering, microlens, random-lens, and wavy structures, complete mixing by the structure increases the spectrally weighted extraction by at most about 2 pp, whereas the fully randomized model predicts up to 19 pp at 550 nm (Fig.~\ref{fig:2}e). A forward-scattering volume recovers 92–99\% of the transmissive gain over a planar interface. The distinction holds for three devices, for spectral weighting, for exit media up to $n$ = 3.5 and for finite substrates. This reference is not a universal upper bound, but it explains the limited gain from faster mixing in common passive structures. A reduced model based on the same reciprocity condition predicts multi-pass extraction with a mean absolute error of 0.30 pp for the structures used in its development and 0.48 pp for seven of nine further structures; it fails for reducible chains, as confirmed prospectively, and is less accurate for faceted structures. On fabricated devices with two emitters, the finite-geometry model predicts the angular factors without adjustment, within 0.03 of the device means, and shows that small lens patches raise the on-axis EQE without raising the total extraction. Larger gains will require mixing placed in transmission or at the device, lens patches much wider than the substrate thickness, or angle-selective structures.
