\textbf{Test statistic.} For each objective the two designs are re-evaluated $n = 3$ times. The statistic is $t = \Delta/(s_p\sqrt{2/n})$, where $\Delta$ is the absolute difference of the means and $s_p$ the pooled standard deviation; the one-sided 95\% threshold at four degrees of freedom is 2.13. The five-repeat re-measurement of the 20–40\ensuremath{^\circ} arm uses eight degrees of freedom (threshold 1.86; 2.90 at 1\%). With a Holm correction over the five objectives, the step-wise one-sided thresholds are 3.75, 3.50, 3.19, 2.78, and 2.13; the 40–60\ensuremath{^\circ}, 60–80\ensuremath{^\circ}, and total-EQE gains ($t$ = 115, 30, 69) remain significant. The large $t$ values reflect the small re-evaluation spread, about $10^{-4}$ in absolute EQE.

\textbf{Why the restart control was needed.} In the original per-band campaign, which was randomly seeded, the 0–20\ensuremath{^\circ} and 20–40\ensuremath{^\circ} gains came out below unity, at 0.946 and 0.966. Because the hemisphere is a point of the 13-variable feasible set, $G_j \ge 1$ holds for an exhaustive search. A value below unity is therefore a statement about the search, not about optics. It was also the strongest objection to the main result: a search that fails to recover a solution it contains cannot be trusted when it reports that nothing better exists. The restart control removes this ambiguity.

\textbf{Protocol.} Each arm's search restarts at that arm's hemispherical optimum: the fixed quarter-circle control points with that arm's optimized cavity thicknesses and lens height. The hemisphere point and eight perturbations within 8\% of each variable's range form the seed set. The search runs three ways: \texttt{surrogateopt} from the seed set, \texttt{patternsearch} from the surrogate winner, and \texttt{patternsearch} launched directly from the hemisphere point. The last is the most direct test of whether the hemisphere is a local optimum in thirteen dimensions. The winner is re-evaluated at final fidelity. The hemisphere baseline is re-measured in the same session at final fidelity. The archived value serves only as a cross-check. An arm counts as improved when the difference exceeds the pooled one-sided 95\% $t$ value at $2(N_{\mathrm{rep}}-1) = 4$ degrees of freedom, 2.13.

\textbf{Reading the control.} The control is one-sided: the hemisphere is among the screened candidates, so the result cannot fall meaningfully below it. It is also biased toward reporting a gain, because the winner is the maximum over roughly seventy noisy search evaluations per arm. The near-zero outcomes are therefore conservative. The two groups differ in scale: residues of +0.29\% to +0.32\%, against margins of +1.01\% to +4.70\% at $t = 30$ to 115. This separation shows that the residues reflect an essentially absent margin rather than a weak search. In the 60–80\ensuremath{^\circ} arm the restart also beat the original campaign's own optimum, so starting at the hemisphere is simply a better search protocol in this design space. The two original sub-unity values were a search artifact, and the corrected values are 1.003 and 1.002.

Three observations follow.

\begin{enumerate}

\item The initial rise saturates with a decay length of about 9 mm, four critical-angle round trips of 2.32 mm each. The far tail does not saturate. Between 35 and 100 mm the total still gains 2.2\%, about three times what a single exponential fitted to the first three points would allow. Light that makes many round trips migrates laterally over many tens of round trips before escaping. The total EQE of a finite film therefore increases slowly with its extent, and the 25 mm value is a lower bound, 3.7\% below the 100 mm value.

\item The angular composition is converged with respect to patch size. While the total gains 3.8\% from 25 to 100 mm, no band selectivity changes by more than 0.24 pp (0.11 pp between 25 and 35 mm). All ratios, correlations, and class comparisons are evaluated at equal patch and are unaffected.

\item The weighted-sweep campaign's archived best total, 0.5556, falls between the 35 mm and 100 mm values of the same design, while the design re-measures at 0.5428 on the 25 mm patch. That campaign's absolute normalization therefore corresponds to a larger effective patch. Its absolute totals are excluded from the $G_j$ comparison (Table S3). Its compositions and correlations, being ratios, are used. Its coarse-fidelity log maximum (0.5591 at 10,000 rays) is not used anywhere.

\end{enumerate}

\section*{Note S7 | Inverted and randomly assembled MLA families}

We next ask whether the result depends on the array construction. We repeat the analysis for the inverted (concave) and the randomly assembled families (Fig. S9), using the same statistics, each computed from the family's own evaluation log. The concave family uses exactly the variable bounds and feasibility rules of main-text Table 1. Three signatures of saturation recur in all three families:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Collapse.} The (total EQE, 40–60\ensuremath{^\circ} EQE) points again collapse onto a line. $R^2$ is 0.94 for the convex per-band campaign, 0.94 for the concave family, and 0.84 for the random family (Fig. S9, left column). The logs differ in sampling density and, for the random family, in fidelity, so we read these values only as evidence that the collapse recurs, not as a ranking of the families.
\item \textbf{Narrow composition.} The concave family's best total EQE is 0.517, 6\% below the convex 0.548, as expected for a recessed rather than protruding surface. Its natural composition is 0.113 / 0.303 / 0.340 / 0.215. The random family reaches 0.522 \ensuremath{\pm} 0.001 over three disorder realizations, a coefficient of variation of 0.2\%. Only the assembly statistics matter, not the particular realization. Its natural composition is 0.111 / 0.299 / 0.347 / 0.213. Band-dedicated optimization of the concave family gives a largest net band-power gain of 1.19, in the 60–80\ensuremath{^\circ} band, close to the convex family's 1.22.
\item \textbf{Same direction of tilt.} Selectivity again rises with efficiency at 0–40\ensuremath{^\circ} and falls at 60–80\ensuremath{^\circ} (Fig. S9, right column). The 40–60\ensuremath{^\circ} correlation is weak in the two periodic families and negative in the random one. In every family the 40–60\ensuremath{^\circ} band remains the largest in at least 99\% of sampled designs.
\end{itemize}

The families also bound how far the composition can be moved at all. Every high-efficiency composition we obtained lies within 0.09–0.11 (0–20\ensuremath{^\circ}), 0.28–0.30 (20–40\ensuremath{^\circ}), 0.34–0.36 (40–60\ensuremath{^\circ}), and 0.21–0.24 (60–80\ensuremath{^\circ}). This spans three constructions, four single-band objectives, and two averaging conventions (top-twenty median and population mean). The change from convex to concave lenslets moves the composition to the edge of this window. The shift is at most 0.02 in any band, about the same as switching the averaging convention within one family (Note S4, Table S6c). Positional disorder and per-lenslet shape disorder leave the composition inside the window. The composition is therefore set by the platform—extended source, thick substrate, coextensive external film—not by any design variable within it.

The randomly assembled MLA should not be confused with a volumetric scattering layer. Each lenslet remains a refractive surface with deterministic local normals and a single index step. A scattering layer instead redirects light by multiple volumetric scattering. Its behaviour depends on the particle–matrix index contrast and loading, which are not parameters of this study. Our conclusions apply to refractive external films and make no comparison with scattering layers.

\section*{Note S8 | Asymmetric three-dimensional freeform exploration}

To check whether axial symmetry itself limits the outcome, three-dimensional asymmetric freeform lenslets were optimized directly against a restricted $(\theta,\phi)$ window, with the objective

\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{EQE}_{\mathrm{win}}=\int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2}\!\!\int_{\phi_1}^{\phi_2} I_{\mathrm{air}}(\theta,\phi)\,\sin\theta\,d\phi\,d\theta .
\end{equation*}

This was a separate, differently constrained study. It used a different OLED stack (Ag rather than ITO electrode), an anisotropic emitter cell, and 52 shape variables rather than 13. It is therefore not part of the same-constraints benchmark.

The asymmetric shapes could shift the centroid and fine structure of the far field. They did not reliably achieve an absolute window power appreciably above the hemispherical MLA. We report this comparison qualitatively and give no ratio. The stack, emitter cell, and parameter count all differ from the controlled benchmark, so a numerical gain would invite a side-by-side reading that the scope does not support. What carries over is the sign of the effect, not its magnitude. Periodic arrays can steer light under other conditions: asymmetric prisms, diffractive elements, metasurfaces, or sufficient aperture expansion can produce directional emission (see, e.g., main ref. 10). Within coextensive refractive MLAs on a uniform extended source, such redistribution did not become a useful output channel beyond the hemispherical reference.

\section*{Note S9 | Angle retention over many passes and the effect of a randomizing rear}

\textbf{Purpose.} This note quantifies why reshaping the lens buys little, by following the light pass by pass, and tests whether breaking the angle retention of the returned light would restore leverage to the lens shape.

\textbf{Method.} The bidirectional scattering distribution functions (BSDFs) of the optimized lenses of this work were computed with a Monte-Carlo height-field ray tracer for a hexagonally close-packed array, index-matched to the substrate ($n = 1.51$). The tracer reproduces the LightTools BSDF of a hemispherical MLA: escape probability 0.4275 against 0.4274, and angle-resolved transmittance within 0.01. The profiles were rebuilt from the optimized control points with a PCHIP spline; LightTools uses its own spline, so the freeform BSDFs approximate the traced geometry. The 0–20\ensuremath{^\circ} freeform optimum is excluded because it ends in a vertical sidewall at the rim, which a height-field tracer cannot represent. The BSDFs enter the angle-resolved multi-pass model of main ref. 11: the light in the substrate is a vector over polar angle, the film transmits $B_T$ and returns $B_R$, and the device reflects with $R(\theta)$ from a CPS/transfer-matrix model of the same stack at 550 nm (Al or Ag cathode, ITO 150 nm, horizontal dipole ratio 0.865). Two device reflections are compared: angle-preserving (the actual device) and a Lambertian reset, in which the returned light is redistributed as $\cos\theta\sin\theta$ at every pass. To separate lens from cavity, all lenses were also evaluated with one common cavity (ETL 61.3 nm, HTL 97.5 nm, the hemispherical total-EQE optimum).

\textbf{Depletion.} With the angle-preserving device, the escape probability of the hemispherical total-EQE design falls pass by pass: 0.43, 0.39, 0.35, 0.31, 0.27, 0.23 (Al) and 0.41, 0.37, 0.34, 0.30, 0.27, 0.24 (Ag). Light at angles the lens transmits well leaves first, and the remainder sits at angles it transmits poorly. With the Lambertian reset the escape probability stays at 0.42.

\section*{Note S10 | Angular mixing number and trapping ratio: definition, robustness, and validation}

\textbf{Definition.} The return matrix $B_R(\theta_i\to\theta_r)$ of a structure gives the fraction of the power incident in polar bin $i$ that re-enters the substrate in bin $r$. Normalizing every column by the returned fraction $1-p(\theta_i)$ gives the column-stochastic kernel $M$ of the conditional Markov chain of the returned light. When transmission or absorption removes light, the surviving light relaxes to a quasi-stationary distribution; normalizing the columns separates this escape from the redistribution itself. For a reciprocal structure $w_i B_R(i\to r) = w_r B_R(r\to i)$ with the étendue weight $w_k = \sin^2\theta_{k+1}-\sin^2\theta_k$, so $D^{1/2} M D^{-1/2}$ is symmetric for a suitable diagonal $D$, and the spectrum of $M$ is real. We define $N_{\mathrm{mix}} = 1/(1-|\lambda_2|)$, with $\lambda_2$ the eigenvalue of second-largest modulus (the absolute spectral gap of main ref. 20). For every structure in this work the second eigenvalue was real and positive and also the second largest in modulus. $N_{\mathrm{mix}}$ is the relaxation time, not the e-folding pass number $-1/\ln|\lambda_2|$; for $\lambda_2$ = 0.5 the two are 2 and 1.44.

\textbf{Grazing-angle truncation.} Bins near 90\ensuremath{^\circ} carry almost no étendue but can return light at their own angle; a few such states can then dominate $\lambda_2$ without affecting extraction. We therefore evaluate $\lambda_2$ on $\theta < 85\ensuremath{^\circ}$, which excludes 0.76\% of the étendue. Table S10 shows the effect.

With the reciprocal reduced model (main-text Sec. 2.3), truncation at 85\ensuremath{^\circ} lowers the mean error over all 342 cases from 0.55 to 0.30 pp and the largest error from 4.7 to 3.0 pp; for freeform lenses the largest error falls from 4.7 to 2.1 pp.

\textbf{Reciprocity of the traced matrices.} Detailed balance requires $F_{ji} = w_i B_R(i\to j)$ to be symmetric. The relative asymmetry $\sum|F-F^{\mathsf T}|/\sum|F|$ is 0.05 (scattering layer, $g$ = 0.8, $\tau^*$ = 1), 0.06 (hemispherical MLA), 0.08 (random lenslet shapes, $s$ = 1.0), 0.10 and 0.13 (wavy surfaces of rms slope 0.2 and 1.0) on 1\ensuremath{^\circ} bins. On 5\ensuremath{^\circ} bins it is 0.009–0.024 and on 15\ensuremath{^\circ} bins 0.002–0.007, falling as expected for Monte Carlo noise; the planar matrix is exactly symmetric.

\textbf{Reciprocal redistribution.} For the reduced kernel $B_R(i\to r) = (1-p_i)[(1-m)\delta_{ir} + m q_r]$, detailed balance of the mixing term requires $w_i(1-p_i)q_r = w_r(1-p_r)q_i$ for all $i, r$, i.e. $q \propto w(1-p)$. The normalized kernel is $(1-m)I + m\,q\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}$, with eigenvalues 1 (eigenvector $q$) and $1-m$ ($n-1$-fold), so $m = 1-\lambda_2$ matches the relaxation rate for either choice of $q$.

\textbf{Late-pass escape probability.} With $D = \mathrm{diag}(w)$, detailed balance means that $D^{-1/2}B_R D^{1/2}$ is symmetric, so $\rho(B_R)$ is the maximum of the Rayleigh quotient $x^{\mathsf T} D^{-1/2}B_R D^{1/2} x / x^{\mathsf T}x$. With $x = D^{1/2}\mathbf{1}$ the quotient is $\sum_i w_i(1-p_i)/\sum_i w_i = 1-p$, which gives $p_\infty \le p$. The column sums of $B_R$ are $1-p(\theta_i)$, so $\rho \le \max(1-p)$ and $p_\infty \ge \min p$. Both are evaluated on $\theta < 85\ensuremath{^\circ}$.

We also tested two scalar uses of $p_\infty$. Fitting a two-parameter reciprocal family ($B_R = \mathrm{diag}(a) + c\,(w g)\,g^{\mathsf T}$ with $g = (1-p)^{\gamma}$, which is reciprocal for any $\gamma$ and reduces to the one-parameter model at $\gamma = 1$) to both $\lambda_2$ and $\rho$ lowers the error for the shallow wavy surfaces (mean 0.5 pp) and scattering layers (0.17 pp) but raises it for MLAs (0.43–0.65 pp); averaged over all cases (0.32 pp) it is no better than the one-parameter reciprocal model. A scalar two-rate formula, with the per-pass escape probability relaxing from its first-pass value to $p_\infty$ at rate $\lambda_2$, reaches 0.5–0.9 pp for lens arrays but fails for the planar interface. Neither is used in the main text, where $p_\infty$ serves as a diagnostic through $\xi$.

\textbf{Validation.} The reduced model was compared with the full series for every structure of the library (Note S11), with the Al, Ag, and low-loss devices at 500, 550, and 600 nm (342 cases). Table S11 summarizes the absolute errors by class, in percentage points, for the reciprocal ($q \propto w(1-p)$) and Lambertian ($q \propto w$) redistribution, both with $m = 1-\lambda_2$, and for the memoryless formula. \section*{Note S11 | Library of structures and return matrices}

\textbf{Height-field tracer.} Microlens arrays, random lens arrays, and wavy surfaces are traced with a Monte-Carlo height-field ray tracer: the surface $z = h(x, y)$ separates the index-matched medium ($n = 1.51$) from air, rays are marched with a Lipschitz-safe step and refined by bisection, and each interface event chooses reflection or refraction by the unpolarized Fresnel coefficients. Rays start at the base plane with a uniform position over the periodic cell and a uniform azimuth; 20,000 rays per 1\ensuremath{^\circ} incidence bin are used (10,000 for wavy surfaces). The transmitted rays are histogrammed by their polar angle in air ($B_T$), the returned rays by their polar angle in the substrate ($B_R$). For a single hemispherical lenslet the tracer reproduces the LightTools return matrix of main ref. 11: escape probability 0.4275 against 0.4274, angle-resolved transmittance within 0.008, and less than 0.1\% of rays undetermined.

\textbf{Random lens arrays.} A 6 \ensuremath{\times} 6 supercell of lenslets is tiled periodically. Every lenslet may carry its own spline profile, its own height, and a jittered centre and radius. Shape spread $s$ perturbs the five control points of the quarter circle by Gaussian noise of standard deviation $0.25s$ (radial) and $0.4s$ (height); $s$ = 1 approaches independently drawn profiles. Where lenslets overlap, the higher surface is taken; gaps are flat. Profiles with near-vertical walls, which a height field cannot represent, are rejected.

\textbf{Wavy surfaces.} Periodic, Gaussian-correlated random height fields $h = h_0 + \sum_m a_m\cos(\mathbf{g}_m\cdot\mathbf{x}+\phi_m)$ on a 12 \ensuremath{\times} 12 box with correlation length one, scaled to a prescribed rms slope. Ray optics is scale-free, so only the slope statistics matter; the physical correlation length must exceed a few wavelengths.

\textbf{Scattering layers.} Non-absorbing, index-matched slabs with a Henyey–Greenstein phase function, Monte Carlo with 20,000 rays per 0.5\ensuremath{^\circ} incidence bin, combined with the flat exit face by the adding method.

\section*{Reproducing the figures and data}

Main-text Figs. 1–4 are produced by \texttt{selectivity/oe\_figs.py}, which builds the structure library (Note S11), evaluates the full and reduced models, and writes each figure to \texttt{figures\_oe/} as PNG (300 dpi), PDF, and an Excel workbook with one sheet per panel holding exactly the plotted arrays. The random-structure return matrices are generated by \texttt{multipass\_reset/run\_random\_sweep.py} (tracer \texttt{hftrace\_gen.py}). Main-text Fig. 5 and Figs. S1–S10 come from the freeform campaign archives through \texttt{make\_figures.py} and \texttt{make\_supp\_figures\_and\_data.py}.

\begin{verbatim}
cd multipass_reset && python3 run_random_sweep.py && cd ..
cd selectivity && python3 extract_inputs.py && python3 run_devices.py && python3 oe_figs.py && cd ..
python3 make_figures.py
python3 make_supp_figures_and_data.py
\end{verbatim}
