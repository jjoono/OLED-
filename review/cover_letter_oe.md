# Cover letter (Optics Express)

[Date]

Dear Editor,

We submit the manuscript "Reciprocity separates reflective and transmissive angular mixing in OLED light extraction" for consideration as a Research Article in *Optics Express*.

Outcoupling structures for OLEDs are usually discussed with the fully randomized model of statistical ray optics, in which light that is mixed in angle escapes with the average escape probability of the structure. Our manuscript shows that optical reciprocity makes this assumption depend on where the light is mixed. An escape interface that mixes light in reflection must return it preferentially to the angles from which it cannot escape, and the escape probability of the mixed light falls by the angular variance of the escape probability divided by the trapped fraction. Mixing in transmission through a weakly absorbing volume has no such penalty and recovers the fully randomized model. Mixing at the device is penalized only if its reflectance is correlated with the escape probability.

The consequences are quantitative. For 37 microlens, scattering, random-lens, and wavy structures in three device stacks, complete mixing by the structure itself adds at most about 2 percentage points to the spectrally weighted extraction, whereas the fully randomized model predicts up to 19 points. A forward-scattering volume under a planar face recovers 92–99% of the fully randomized gain. A one-parameter reduced model built on the reciprocity condition reproduces 342 angle-resolved calculations with a mean error of 0.30 percentage points. We also report where it fails: for angular chains with closed sets, which we predicted before computing the test cases, and with reduced accuracy for faceted structures. Fabricated devices with hemispherical and freeform microlens arrays on two emitters show the predicted angular redistribution without adjusted parameters and demonstrate that on-axis metrics overstate the benefit of lens patches that are small compared with the substrate thickness.

We believe the work fits *Optics Express* because it gives designers of light-extraction and light-recycling structures a simple rule, backed by ray-optical transport calculations, direct ray tracing, and measurements, for deciding whether angular mixing is worth pursuing and where it should be placed. The argument uses only reciprocity and étendue, so it also applies to LEDs, luminescent converters, and other slabs with an escape face and a back reflector; the manuscript includes results for exit media with refractive indices up to 3.5.
