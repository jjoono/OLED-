## Abstract

Organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs) generate photons with internal quantum efficiencies close to unity, yet only 20–30% of these photons typically leave the device. External outcoupling structures and high-refractive-index substrates raise the external quantum efficiency (EQE) to about 60%, where progress has stalled. Here we show that this ceiling is set not by the outcoupling structure but by parasitic absorption during the repeated round trips that photons make between the outcoupling surface and the rear of the device. We derive a closed-form photon-recycling law that expresses the extraction efficiency in terms of the single-pass escape probability and the round-trip loss of the OLED stack, and show that extraction approaches unity as the round-trip loss vanishes, irrespective of emitter orientation, layer thicknesses and lens geometry. The law reorders the design rules of outcoupled OLEDs: internal absorption in transparent electrodes and transport layers, negligible in conventional devices, becomes the dominant loss. Guided by the law, a supplementary rear reflector placed outside the electrical path raises the measured EQE of a phosphorescent OLED on a high-index microlens substrate from 61% to 77%, and a silver rear reflector yields a measured extraction efficiency of 91.6% of the substrate-delivered light on a conventional microlens film, with the measured peak EQE (55.8%) matching the model (55.9%).

## Main text

Organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs) convert injected charges into photons with internal quantum efficiencies close to 100% using phosphorescent or thermally activated delayed-fluorescence emitters [1,2]. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) of a planar device nevertheless remains at 20–30%, because most photons are trapped in substrate-guided and waveguided modes or are lost to surface plasmon polaritons (SPPs) at the metal electrode [3–5]. Three decades of work have addressed these channels through microcavity tuning [6], horizontally oriented emitters [7], and internal or external outcoupling structures such as microlens arrays (MLAs), scattering layers, corrugations and low-index grids [8–11]. Combining a high-refractive-index substrate with an external extraction structure recovers both substrate and waveguide modes [12,13], and globally optimized devices of this kind reach EQEs of about 60% [13,14]. Notably, this value has been reached by rather different optical designs, and progress beyond it has been slow, which suggests a common limitation rather than a shortcoming of any particular structure.

Here we identify that limitation and show how to remove it. With an extraction structure in place, only a fraction of the substrate-delivered light escapes on its first encounter with the outcoupling surface; the remainder is returned into the OLED, reflected at the rear electrode and offered to the outcoupling surface again. Every such round trip costs a fraction of the photons to absorption in the rear metal, in the transparent conductive oxide (TCO) and in the organic layers. A high-index substrate suppresses waveguiding but also lowers the escape probability per pass, so photons make more round trips and the per-pass losses compound. We show that the extraction efficiency is governed by a single figure of merit, the round-trip loss of the OLED stack, and derive a closed-form law which predicts that extraction approaches unity as this loss vanishes, independent of emitter orientation, layer thicknesses and the detailed geometry of the extraction structure. The law reorders the design priorities of outcoupled OLEDs, and we validate it with two sets of devices. (Reflector-based recovery of trapped light has recently also been announced by industry [15]; neither a quantitative design theory nor measured extraction efficiencies have been disclosed.)

**A closed-form photon-recycling law.** Consider the power P_0 delivered by the OLED into the substrate, with angular distribution P_sub(θ) (Fig. 1a). The outcoupling surface transmits the fraction B_T(θ) to air and reflects the remainder back with an angular redistribution described by the reflection part of its bidirectional scattering distribution function (BSDF), B_R(θ_i, θ_r). The returned light is reflected by the OLED stack with the angle- and wavelength-dependent reflectance R_LED(θ), and the process repeats. Summing the escaping power over all orders of recycling gives

P_out = P_0 · P_sub · [ I + B_R R + (B_R R)^2 + … ] · B_T^T = P_0 · P_sub · (I − B_R R)^(−1) · B_T^T,   (1)

where P_sub and B_T are row vectors over the angle of incidence, B_R is a matrix and R is the reflectance written as a diagonal matrix (Methods; Supplementary Section 1). Equation (1) is what our trans-scale model evaluates numerically. Its physics is exposed by replacing the angular quantities by their averages over the recycled light, p ≡ ⟨P_sub · B_T⟩ (the escape probability per pass) and A′ ≡ 1 − ⟨R_LED⟩ (the parasitic loss per round trip), and by using B_R ≈ 1 − B_T. The geometric series then sums to

η_ext ≡ P_out / P_0 ≈ p / [ p + (1 − p) A′ ],   (2)

and the EQE of the device is the product

EQE = η_sub^(0) · η_ext,   (3)

where η_sub^(0) is the fraction of the internally generated photons that reaches the substrate in the first place (the conventional substrate-mode efficiency, including the directly outcoupled air mode).

Equation (2) is plotted in Fig. 1b. For a hemispherical MLA of the same index as the substrate, p ≈ 0.4 (n = 1.5), so a round-trip loss of 30%, typical for an aluminium cathode combined with a TCO, limits η_ext to about 0.7, whereas a loss of 3–5% raises it to 0.93–0.96, and η_ext → 1 as A′ → 0 for any p > 0. The sensitivity, dη_ext/dA′ = −(1 − p)/p at A′ = 0, is largest precisely where p is small, that is, for the high-index substrates that are needed to suppress waveguiding. Two consequences follow. First, the reflectance of the rear of the device, not the geometry of the extraction structure, sets the ceiling of an outcoupled OLED; a lossy rear reflector cannot be compensated by a better lens. Second, the internal absorption of the stack, which is a negligible loss channel in a conventional OLED where photons traverse it only once, becomes the dominant loss once an extraction structure forces multiple passes. The design rules of an outcoupled OLED therefore differ from those of a conventional one.

**Why previous devices stalled at 60%.** Figure 1c shows the EQE of a globally optimized bottom-emitting phosphorescent OLED (Methods) on a hemispherical-MLA substrate as a function of the substrate/MLA refractive index, computed with the full model of Eq. (1). With an aluminium cathode, the EQE rises to about 60% at n ≈ 1.65–1.7 and then decreases with increasing index, even though the substrate-mode efficiency η_sub^(0) continues to grow, because the falling escape probability p multiplies the number of lossy round trips; the published record values [13,14] sit at the maximum of this curve. Replacing the aluminium cathode by a low-loss rear reflector — a transparent IZO electrode backed by a distributed Bragg reflector (DBR), or a thick silver electrode [[add computed Ag curve using measured n,k of Ag]] — removes the trade-off: the EQE increases monotonically with the substrate index and exceeds 80% at n ≈ 1.8. The loss budget (Supplementary Fig. S2) confirms that in the aluminium device on a high-index MLA substrate, parasitic absorption during recycling is the single largest loss channel.

**A supplementary rear reflector raises the measured EQE from 61% to 77%.** The simplest embodiment of the law leaves the OLED itself unchanged and adds a low-loss mirror outside the electrical path. We fabricated bottom-emitting phosphorescent OLEDs (Ir(dmppy-ph)_2tmd emitter; layer thicknesses optimized by [[particle swarm / genetic]] optimization; Methods) on a high-index (n = 1.77) hemispherical-MLA substrate and covered the rear of the finished device, including the area surrounding the aluminium cathode, with a 4.5-pair ZnS/LiF DBR deposited by thermal evaporation (Fig. 2a). In such devices a substantial part of the substrate-guided light propagates laterally beyond the cathode footprint and would exit through the rear surface: the reference device without a reflector lost 18.6% of its luminance through the backside, which the DBR reduced to 0.9% (Supplementary Table S1). Correspondingly, the peak EQE increased from 23% for a reference device [[confirm: same stack on a flat substrate without MLA]] to 61% with the MLA substrate and to 77% (exp.) with the MLA substrate and the supplementary DBR (Fig. 2b; peak at [[x]] cd m^−2; [[xx]]% at 1,000 cd m^−2; N = [[x]] devices). The increment of 16 percentage points is consistent, to first order, with recycling of the recovered ≈18% into a device whose extraction efficiency for recycled light is about 0.85 [[check]], and it is obtained without any change to the OLED stack.

**Ninety-two per cent of the substrate light extracted with a silver reflector.** The reflector need not be a dielectric mirror. In a second device set (Ir(ppy)_2acac emitter on a conventional glass substrate with an n ≈ 1.5 MLA film; Methods) we deposited a MoOx/Ag (5 nm/100 nm) reflector on top of a 3-µm parylene-C encapsulation layer, again covering the full rear surface outside the electrical path (Fig. 2c). The device reached a peak EQE of 55.8% (exp.) at [[x]] cd m^−2, compared with [[≈18]]% for the reference without the MLA film (Fig. 2d). The trans-scale model, with all layer thicknesses and the measured optical constants (including the extinction of the emitting layer) as input, predicts 55.9% for the same structure. The model further gives the power delivered into the substrate, η_sub^(0) = 0.609, from which the measured outcoupled power of 0.558 corresponds to an extraction efficiency η_ext = 0.558/0.609 = 91.6% of the substrate-delivered light (exp./sim.; Methods). This compares with 75.4% for the best previously reported photon-management scheme evaluated in the same way [13], and demonstrates near-unity extraction on a commodity low-index microlens film once the round-trip loss is suppressed.

**Design rules of an outcoupled OLED.** Because Eq. (2) contains neither layer thicknesses nor the emitter orientation, the law predicts that the dependence of the EQE on these parameters should largely disappear once a low-loss reflector and an extraction structure are combined. Figure 3 tests this with the full model for a TADF (SpiroAC-TRZ) device with a thick silver electrode on an n = 1.77 MLA substrate. (a) Without the MLA, the EQE map versus electron- and hole-transport-layer thicknesses shows the familiar Fabry–Pérot pattern with a maximum of 38.7%; with the MLA, the map is flat above d_ETL ≈ 100 nm with a maximum of 87.6%, and the only residual dependences are the parasitic loss of the thicker layers and SPP coupling at small electron-transport-layer thickness (the substrate-mode efficiency grows from 73.7% to 96.8% as both transport layers are thickened from 50 nm to 500 nm). (b) The EQE varies by less than 3 percentage points (≈87–89.5%) across horizontal dipole fractions Θ = 0.5–1, including the isotropic case (Θ = 0.67), whereas the same emitter series on a conventional n = 1.5 substrate with an aluminium cathode spans 25–49%. Neither microcavity tuning nor oriented emitters are therefore prerequisites for high efficiency in this regime. (c) In return, the EQE becomes acutely sensitive to internal absorption: raising the extinction coefficient of the transparent electrode from 0 to 0.02 lowers the achievable EQE from 96% to about 80%, and increasing the real part of the refractive index of the silver mirror from its bulk value to 0.5 (representative of alloyed or thin-film metals) lowers it from 95% to 46%. The same accounting explains the ranking of electrode-integrated reflectors: for thickness-optimized structures, the model gives 86.6% for a thick silver electrode but 82.9% for an IZO electrode backed by a DBR, because double-pass absorption in the TCO (≈[[4–9]]% per round trip for k_IZO = [[0.02–0.04]]) outweighs the higher reflectance of the dielectric mirror, and at oblique incidence the DBR's reflectance is in any case total internal reflection at its first low-index layer, which any low-index cladding provides. The choice of reflector is thus a quantitative design decision made by Eq. (1), not a matter of principle; what is essential is a low round-trip loss, which requires a high-reflectance rear surface, transparent (low-k) electrodes and thick, low-absorption transport layers.

**Outlook.** The law indicates a route to EQEs beyond 90%: for the integrated silver-electrode device on the n = 1.77 MLA substrate, the model predicts 86.6% with the measured emitter parameters and 90.5% for an emitter with unity photoluminescence quantum yield, the remaining losses being absorption in the TCO and in the metal. Supplementary rear reflectors, as used here, require substrate area beyond the electrode footprint and are therefore best suited to lighting, photobiomodulation and other large-area sources; in pixelated displays the same principle must be implemented by the reflective electrode itself, which sets the electrode requirements quantified in Fig. 3c. Because Eqs. (1)–(3) contain no property specific to organic semiconductors, the same law governs any planar emitter combined with an external extraction structure.

## Methods

**Trans-scale optical model.** Step 1 (wave optics): emission in the planar OLED stack is computed with a transfer-matrix dipole model in the Poynting-vector formulation, which treats the emitting layer as an ensemble of oscillating dipoles with horizontal dipole fraction Θ and radiative efficiency q, uses the measured complex refractive indices of all layers (including the extinction coefficient of the emitting layer, which is required to reproduce measured EQEs; Supplementary Fig. S5) and optionally birefringence, and yields the power delivered into the substrate P_sub(θ, λ), the modal power distribution (air, substrate, waveguide, SPP, absorption) and the reflectance R_LED(θ, λ) of the stack for light incident from the substrate. Step 2 (ray optics): the outcoupling structure is characterized by its BSDF, computed by ray tracing [[in-house / LightTools]] for a hexagonally close-packed hemispherical MLA of refractive index n_MLA and, where indicated, for scattering layers; azimuthal symmetry reduces the BSDF to the vector B_T(θ_i) and the matrix B_R(θ_i, θ_r) used in Eq. (1). The outcoupled power, EQE, current efficiency and angular emission follow from Eq. (1), evaluated per wavelength and weighted by the emission spectrum (Supplementary Section 1). Layer thicknesses are optimized by particle swarm optimization [[or a genetic algorithm for the DBR device set — confirm]] in MATLAB. Emitter parameters: [[q and Θ for Ir(dmppy-ph)_2tmd, Ir(ppy)_2acac and SpiroAC-TRZ]]. The model is described in ref. [16] and was validated previously against MLA and scattering-layer devices [13,17].

**Closed-form law.** Approximating the angular quantities in Eq. (1) by their averages over the recycled light, writing B_R ≈ 1 − B_T (the outcoupling surface neither absorbs nor leaks light by other means) and R_LED = 1 − A′, the series in Eq. (1) becomes geometric with ratio (1 − p)(1 − A′), which yields Eq. (2). Supplementary Section 1 gives the derivation and the exact matrix form; Supplementary Fig. S1 shows that EQE values from Eq. (2) agree with the full model to within [[x]] percentage points for the devices of this work.

**Devices with a DBR rear reflector.** [[Confirm all thicknesses and conditions.]] High-index MLA substrates (HNPS-01, n = 1.77, Lumtec) were coated with an IZO bottom electrode by RF magnetron sputtering ([[conditions]]). Organic layers were deposited by thermal evaporation at a base pressure below 10^−7 mbar in the order TAPC/HAT-CN/TAPC/HAT-CN (p-doped hole-transport stack, [[thicknesses]]), TCTA ([[x]] nm), TCTA:B3PyMPM:Ir(dmppy-ph)_2tmd ([[x]] nm, [[ratio]]), B3PyMPM ([[x]] nm), Liq ([[x]] nm) and Al ([[100]] nm). The supplementary DBR, 4.5 pairs of ZnS/LiF with layer thicknesses optimized by [[PSO/GA]] for the emission spectrum, was thermally evaporated over the complete rear surface of the finished device through an open mask. Reference devices were fabricated on flat [[glass/ITO]] substrates. Active area [[x]] mm^2.

**Devices with a silver rear reflector.** [[Confirm.]] On ITO-coated glass (150 nm), HAT-CN/TAPC (5/55 nm, three pairs), TCTA (10 nm), TCTA:B3PyMPM:Ir(ppy)_2acac (25 nm), B3PyMPM (50 nm), B3PyMPM:Cs_2CO_3 (5 nm) and Ag (100 nm) were deposited by thermal evaporation. The devices were encapsulated with an [[Al_2O_3/… nanolaminate]] and 3 µm of parylene-C, after which MoOx (5 nm) and Ag (100 nm) were evaporated over the full rear surface. A commercial hemispherical MLA film (n ≈ 1.5, [[supplier]]) was laminated to the glass with an index-matching [[fluid/adhesive]].

**Characterization.** Current–voltage–luminance characteristics were recorded with a source-measure unit (Keithley 2400), and the total emitted radiant flux was measured in an integrating sphere ([[diameter, model]]) with a calibrated spectrometer ([[model]]); the EQE was calculated from the spectrally resolved flux and the current. Emission from the substrate edges was [[blocked with black tape / included]]. Backside leakage was measured as L_back/(L_front + L_back) at 5 V with a [[luminance meter / photodiode]] positioned [[geometry]] on either side of the device. Optical constants were determined by spectroscopic ellipsometry. [[N]] devices of each type were measured; quoted values are [[champion / mean ± s.d.]].

**Definition of the extraction efficiency.** η_ext is the outcoupled power divided by the power delivered into the substrate by the OLED stack. For the silver-reflector device the outcoupled power is the measured EQE (0.558) and the substrate-delivered power is taken from the model (0.609), as in ref. [13]; η_ext is therefore a hybrid experimental/model quantity, whose model input is validated by the agreement between measured and simulated EQE (55.8% versus 55.9%).
